# The CapEx Wave

Capital spending is money laid out for things that last. Land. Sheds. Power gear. Racks of chips. In the trade it gets shortened to capex. The AI build is, before all else, a capex story.

## What the wave weighs

Four US firms sit at the heart of it: Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet and Meta. Their stated plans for 2026 add up to about $718 billion.[^1] We would not be shocked by anything from $655 billion to $790 billion. Plans slip. Plans also get raised.

The parts: Amazon has told the market to expect about $200 billion.[^2] Alphabet lifted its range to $195 to $205 billion on July 22, 2026, its second raise of the year.[^3] Microsoft has pointed at roughly $190 billion for the calendar year.[^4] Meta has flagged $125 to $145 billion.[^5]

Set that beside 2022. Those four plus Oracle spent about $162 billion that whole year.[^6] By 2025, Amazon alone spent $131.8 billion, Alphabet $91.4 billion, and Meta $72.2 billion.[^7] The line is bending upward, fast.

The wave is wider than four firms. Oracle spent $55.7 billion in the year to May 2026. The chip renter CoreWeave plans $30 to $35 billion this year.[^8] Add China, smaller cloud firms and state programs. One tracker sees world data center spending pass $1 trillion in 2026. Our best guess is about $1.07 trillion. The honest range is $850 billion to $1.28 trillion, because "data center" means different things in different reports.[^9]

Buyers have made matching pledges. OpenAI has announced compute deals worth roughly $1.25 trillion across the next decade. Those are plans, not signed cash, and they get restated often. Anthropic has disclosed about $185 billion.[^10]

One warning before we go on. Dollars are not the same thing as compute. Memory prices rose sharply into 2026, and Microsoft has said openly that part of its higher number is that price rise, not extra machines. So the spend curve is steeper than the capacity curve. Anyone reading capex as a clean measure of how fast supply is arriving will read it a little too high.

## What the money buys

Roughly 58 cents of each dollar buys servers and chips. The rest buys land, sheds, power and network gear. That share is not tightly known. A fair range is 45% to 70%.[^11]

The split matters. A concrete shed and a power line last fifteen years or more. A chip does not. So the bulk of the wave sits in the part that goes stale first.

## How depreciation works

A firm that buys a chip does not charge the whole cost to this year's profit. It spreads the cost across the years it thinks the chip will earn. That spreading is called depreciation. The span of years is the useful life.

The math is blunt. Halve the life and the yearly charge doubles. Stretch the life and profit rises now, while the same cost waits for later. Nothing about the chip changes. Only the call on how long it earns.

That call is a judgement, made by the firm. It is disclosed, so we can read it.

## Everyone stretched. One firm did not.

As the build began, buyers stretched their lives. Microsoft moved servers and network gear from four years to six, starting in its 2023 fiscal year. Alphabet uses six years. Meta moved most servers to 5.5 years in 2025. CoreWeave, whose whole trade is renting chips, also uses six.[^12] Add up the first-year gain from four of these moves and you get about $13.7 billion of added profit. That sum is rough, since the moves are not strictly alike.[^13]

One firm went the other way. Amazon cut part of its server and network fleet from six years to five, from January 2025. Its filing gave the reason flatly: the pace of change in AI and machine learning.[^14] It is the only reversal in the group, and it came from the biggest buyer.

## Why the life call matters so much

The life call does two jobs at once, which is why it carries so much weight.

The first job is profit. Stretch the life and this year's earnings rise, on the same fleet, doing the same work. The second job is the hurdle. A chip must earn back its price before its life runs out. A six-year life sets a low yearly bar. A three-year life sets a bar twice as high. Chip rental prices are falling as new supply lands, so the bar and the price are moving toward each other. Where they cross is the whole game.

Nobody outside these firms knows the true earning life of an AI chip. Views are far apart.

The short case: in late 2025 the investor Michael Burry said the group would understate depreciation by $176 billion over 2026 to 2028, and named Oracle and Meta. Treat that as a contested charge, not a fact. He has not shown his working, and others who rebuilt it get much smaller numbers.[^15]

The long case: CoreWeave says its 2020-era chips are still fully booked, and that one lapsing batch of H100 chips was re-signed at about 95% of the old price.[^16] That is one deal, told by the seller. It still shows old chips earning real money years on.

A middle reading comes from Epoch AI. It tracked how long leading chip designs stay in use for frontier training. The median is about 3.9 years, with a fair range of 2.4 to 4.5.[^17] So: a short window at the front, where a chip trains the best models, then a longer tail serving users. The fight is over what that tail is worth.

## The payback question

Prices give a clue. Rental rates for H100 chips fell steeply once new supply landed. Our best guess is a fall of about 60% from the 2023 peak. The range is wide, 28% to 82%, because "the" price is really several prices. Big cloud firms charge far more per chip-hour than small renters do, and much of the drop is buyers moving down a tier rather than one price falling.[^18]

Payback moved with it. SemiAnalysis reckons a chip put to work in 2023 paid back its cost in roughly two years. Call it 1.9, with a range of 1.3 to 4.[^19] A chip bought at late-2025 prices did not clear that bar on short deals. Same chip, later year, worse answer.

Then the largest question of all. David Cahn of Sequoia asks what revenue the build must earn to be worth it. He doubles yearly data center spending to allow for margin. For 2026 he lands near $1.5 trillion of lifetime revenue, set against one year of building.[^20] AI revenue today is nowhere near that.

## What we think, and how firmly

The build is real, huge, and mostly made of things that go stale. That much is solid.

The life call is where the answer hides. If chips earn well for six years, the books are broadly honest and the payback math is hard but doable. If they earn well for three, a large slice of a trillion-dollar year has to be repaid in a rush.

Our view: four to five years of good earning per chip, with a weak tail after. We hold it loosely. It is the one call that moves the answer most, and the people best placed to settle it are the same people who made it.
