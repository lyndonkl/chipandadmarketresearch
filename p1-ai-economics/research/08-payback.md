# The Payback Question

The money is spent. The sheds are up. The chips are racked. One question is left, and it is easy to ask.

Does it come back?

Payback means one exact thing here. A firm that builds must earn enough to cover the cost of the kit, plus the cost of the money used to buy it. Cash has a price, whether it is borrowed or raised. Clear that price and the build was worth doing. Miss it and value was burned, even if every chip ran flat out the whole time.

## The size of the bill

Four US firms plan to spend about $718 billion in 2026. We would not be shocked by anything from $655 billion to $790 billion.[^1] Add the rest of the world and one tracker sees total data center spending near $1.07 trillion, on an honest range of $850 billion to $1.28 trillion.[^2] Roughly 58 cents in each dollar buys chips and servers, the part that goes stale fastest.[^3]

Now the other side. One much-quoted rule of thumb holds that a year of building on this scale needs about $1.5 trillion of revenue from real end customers to be worth doing.[^4] Treat that as a rule, not a reading. It rests on guesses about margins, and the honest range runs from $1.1 trillion to $4 trillion. A second house puts the 2030 need near $2 trillion a year and thinks the world falls short by roughly $800 billion.[^5] A third counts $2.9 trillion of building through 2028 and says $1.5 trillion of it must come from lenders, not profits.[^6]

Set that beside what the two US leaders take in now. OpenAI booked $13.07 billion of revenue in 2025 and lost $20.92 billion running the business.[^7] Its run rate was about $24 billion by early 2026.[^8] Anthropic said its run rate crossed $47 billion in May 2026.[^9] It also told investors it would turn a small profit of $559 million in the June quarter, its first.[^10] That last number is a company figure, not an audited one, and it leaves out share-based pay. Both firms grow fast. Both are still small next to the bill.

## What our panel says

We put the question to a panel of forecasters. Will the 2024 to 2026 wave of US AI building earn back at least the cost of its money by the end of 2030? Not "will AI matter". Will this batch of spending pay?

The middle answer was 56 percent. The blended figure came out near 60 percent. Round it either way and you get the same reading. It is close to a coin toss.

That is the headline. The spread under it is the useful part.

## The disagreement is the signal

Three forecasters worked apart. They landed at 27 percent, 56 percent and 61 percent. Where they split is where the real argument sits.

The low voice started from history. Look at past booms in shared plumbing: rail, the fiber glut, shale, solar. Take the batch bought at or near the peak. Only about two in ten of those earned the cost of their money inside five years. Then look at a second set: rich firms adding capacity to a business that is already sold out. Those pay back about seven times in ten. This wave sits across both sets. The low voice put more of the money in the first set, and marked it down.

The middle voice worked from unit economics, meaning the cash one machine earns against what it cost. Its key move: much of this capacity is already sold under long contracts, at prices struck while compute was scarce. A contract locks the price in for the owner. If prices fall later, the pain lands on the firm reselling tokens, not the firm that owns the boxes.

The high voice went after the low voice's arithmetic. There are two ways to credit this batch of kit with future revenue. One spreads that revenue evenly across every machine ever built, which charges these machines the average return of a much larger, later fleet. The other credits them with the fat years they actually had, while they were the only game in town. That single choice moves the answer by about 25 points. It is not settled.

All three agreed on the biggest single risk, and that agreement is worth more than their point estimates. OpenAI has announced compute deals worth roughly $1.25 trillion.[^11] It stands behind a large share of the contracts that shelter everyone else. If those deals are torn up and rewritten, the shelter goes with them, and the low answer wins.

## How the panel works

The method is plain enough to say in one go. Each forecaster first picks reference classes: sets of past events that look like this one, so history supplies a starting rate before anyone argues about today. Each then adjusts for what is truly new, and gives a number with a range. We take the middle of the panel and push it a little away from 50 percent. That last step is called extremizing, and it corrects for the way judges who work apart tend to hedge toward the middle. It helps when a panel is split. It misleads when a panel agrees, because it can shove the group past every member of it. On one question below it did just that, so there we quote the plain middle.

## Three questions underneath

The panel also priced the pieces.

Will top-end US inference sell at or below 1.5 times what it costs to serve by the end of 2028? That is the strict form of the convergence claim. The panel said 13 percent, and this time all three agreed: 12, 13 and 13. Their reason was the same one three times over. Cheap compute cuts the price and the cost of serving together, so it does not by itself squeeze the gap between them. Only rivals selling the very same model do that, and closed weights cannot be copied.

What happens to the flagship price? The panel's middle guess is that the top-tier input price at the end of 2028 sits near 58 percent of its mid-2026 level. The honest range is huge: 17 percent to 195 percent. Much of that width is not about the world but about the words. Which model counts as flagship in 2028, and at whose price, can move the answer twofold on its own.

How big do the two leaders get? Their combined run rate at the end of 2028 lands near $224 billion, on a range of $128 billion to $410 billion. Even the low end has them more than doubling from here.

## What would settle it

Three things are worth watching.

Chip life. Leading chip designs serve at the frontier for about 3.9 years, on a fair range of 2.5 to 4.5.[^12] The books say longer. Microsoft spreads server cost over six years.[^13] Amazon is the only one of the group to go the other way, cutting part of its fleet to five years and naming the pace of AI as the reason.[^14] One short seller claims the group will understate the yearly charge by about $176 billion across 2026 to 2028. That is his estimate of a wrong, not a measured one.[^15]

Second-hand value. Against him, one chip renter re-signed an expiring batch of H100 chips at about 95 percent of the old price, and said its 2020-era chips were still fully booked.[^16] Old kit is still earning.

Timing. A machine bought early, while compute was scarce, paid for itself in about two years.[^17] A machine bought late buys into a thinner market. Same chip, different fate.

## Where we land

Our best estimate is that this batch of spending pays back, on odds a bit better than even. We would not be surprised by anything from one in four to two in three. Anyone quoting a firm answer in either direction is selling something.

The honest position is that the result turns on things nobody has decided yet. Whether one very large buyer stays solvent. Whether power arrives on time. And how a future set of analysts chooses to score it, which is a judgement, not a fact.
