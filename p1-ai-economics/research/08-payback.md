# The Payback Question

The money is spent. The sheds are up. The chips are racked. And far more is coming. One question sits over all of it, and it is easy to ask.

Does it come back?

## One question, not one batch

There is an old way to ask this and a better one. The old way picks a single batch of kit, the chips bought from 2024 to 2026, and asks whether that batch pays. We are done asking it that way. The better question is bigger and, oddly, simpler. Take the whole thing. Every dollar the US AI industry spends, from the first server in 2024 to the last one in 2030, plus the years of earnings that follow. Does the whole program pay?

The swap does more than widen the frame. It clears an old fight off the table. When you score just one batch of chips, you have to decide which sales to credit to it: the fat early years when it had no rivals, or a thin average shared out across every machine built later. That one choice moved the old answer by about 25 points, and no one could settle it. Take the whole industry at once and the choice is gone. There is one pool of money going in and one pool coming out. Nothing to split. Nothing to argue over. What is left is a single hard forecast: how much will this industry earn, at what margin, for how long.

So the hard part did not go away. It moved. It is now entirely about the future.

## What "pays" means

"Pays" has a strict meaning here. It is not enough to earn the cash back. The money used to build had a price of its own. Borrowed, it costs interest. Raised from owners, it costs the return they gave up by not putting it somewhere else. That price goes on the bill too. A build pays only if it earns back the cash and that extra price on top: the nine or ten cents a year, on each dollar, that the same money could have earned elsewhere at similar risk. Clear that bar and real value was made. Miss it and value was burned, even if every chip ran flat out the whole time.

## The size of the bill

Four US firms plan to spend about $718 billion in 2026. We would not be shocked by anything from $655 billion to $790 billion.[^1] Add the rest of the world and one tracker sees total data center spending near $1.07 trillion, on an honest range of $850 billion to $1.28 trillion.[^2] Roughly 58 cents in each dollar buys chips and servers, the part that goes stale fastest.[^3]

Now stack up the yearly bills, 2024 through 2030. For the US industry the total lands somewhere between $4.5 trillion and $5.6 trillion. That is the sum the whole program has to earn back, with the price of money on top.

Others have asked a rougher version of this. One much-quoted rule holds that a year of building on this scale needs about $1.5 trillion of revenue from real end customers to be worth doing.[^4] A second house puts the 2030 need near $2 trillion a year and thinks the world falls short by roughly $800 billion.[^5] A third counts $2.9 trillion of building through 2028 and says $1.5 trillion of it must come from lenders, not profits.[^6]

Set that beside what the two US leaders take in now. OpenAI booked $13.07 billion of revenue in 2025 and lost $20.92 billion running the business.[^7] Its run rate was about $24 billion by early 2026.[^8] Anthropic said its run rate crossed $47 billion in May 2026.[^9] It also told investors it would turn a small profit of $559 million in one quarter, its first.[^10] Both firms grow fast. Both are still small next to the bill.

## Three models

We built three cash-flow models of the whole program. This is the same tool a firm uses to weigh any long project. Lay out the cash in and the cash out, year by year. Then dock each future dollar a little, to pay for the wait and the risk. One model is glum, one is middling, one is sunny. They split on four dials: how fast revenue grows, what margin the industry keeps, how long the kit lasts, and the price of money.

The glum model has revenue reach a $560 billion yearly rate in 2030, a 32 percent margin, and money priced at 11 percent. It loses $2.3 trillion. It does not pay.

The middle model has revenue reach a $740 billion rate, a 43 percent margin, and money at 9.5 percent. It clears the bar by $487 billion. It pays, but only just. For every dollar sunk it hands back about $1.11.

The sunny model has revenue reach a $1 trillion rate, a 55 percent margin, and money at 8.5 percent. It clears by about $900 billion. It pays.

So the full spread runs from minus $2.3 trillion to plus $0.9 trillion. Two of the three models pay. That sounds like fair odds. It is not, and why it is not is the whole finding.

## The panel

We also put the plain question to a panel of forecasters. Not "will AI matter." Will the whole US AI program clear the price of its money, counting the years past 2030? The middle answer was 52 percent. The three forecasters landed at 44, 53 and 52 percent. Round any of them and you get the same reading. A coin toss, tilted a hair toward yes.

That is the headline. Why they all crowd the middle is the useful part.

## The finding is in the tail

Here is the one thing all three models and all three forecasters agree on. Every single year from 2024 to 2030, the industry spends more than it earns. Cash out beats cash in, straight through the build. So none of the payoff comes from those years. All of it comes after: from a long tail of earnings past 2030, once the building slows and the machines finally run for profit.

That tail is the whole game. Strip it out, score only the years through 2030, and even the sunny model loses $3.3 trillion. Without the tail, every model is deep underwater. So the real question is not about the chips bought so far at all. It is about what a grown-up AI industry earns, year after year, once the dust has settled. Does that steady business clear nine or ten percent a year on the money tied up in it?

The middle model says: barely, and maybe not. Its own steady return lands near 8 percent, below the 9.5 percent price of its money. Its $487 billion of value comes from one burst of growth in the early 2030s, not from a business that out-earns its cost of money for good. Nudge one dial the wrong way and it flips. If revenue merely levels off at its 2030 rate instead of climbing, the middle case drops from plus $487 billion to about minus $1.5 trillion.

This is why the room below the middle is so much larger than the room above it. From the middle case down to the glum one is a fall of nearly $2.8 trillion. From the middle up to the sunny one is a rise of just $0.4 trillion. The downside is seven times the upside. A panel staring at that shape has no honest way to sound sure. Hence 52 percent.

## The dial that matters most

If the whole answer lives in the tail, it lives above all in one number: the margin a grown-up AI industry gets to keep. Today the two US leaders run company-wide margins in the low forties, OpenAI near 43 percent[^11] and Anthropic near 40 percent.[^12] On the act of serving a model alone, stripped of the cost of training it, one estimate puts Anthropic above 70 percent.[^13] The glum model assumes rivals grind that margin down to 32 percent. The sunny model assumes it holds and climbs to 55 percent. Each five points of margin is worth roughly half a trillion dollars of value. Nothing else moves the answer as much.

## What could break it

Three things are worth watching, because each feeds the margin and the tail.

Chip life. Leading chip designs serve at the frontier for about 3.9 years, on a fair range of 2.5 to 4.5.[^14] The books say longer. Microsoft spreads server cost over six years.[^15] Amazon is the only one of the group to go the other way, cutting part of its fleet to five years and naming the pace of AI as the reason.[^16] One short seller claims the group will understate the yearly wear charge by about $176 billion across 2026 to 2028. That is his estimate of a wrong, not a measured one.[^17] Against him, one chip renter re-signed an expiring batch of H100 chips at about 95 percent of the old price, and said its 2020-era chips were still fully booked.[^18] Old kit is still earning. A machine bought early, while compute was scarce, paid for itself in about two years.[^19] A machine bought late buys into a thinner market.

The one buyer. OpenAI has announced compute deals worth roughly $1.25 trillion.[^20] It stands behind a large share of the contracts that shelter everyone else. If those deals are torn up and rewritten, the shelter goes with them, and the glum answer gains ground. It is the single largest thing that could tip the whole program.

## Where we land

Our best estimate is that the whole program pays back, on odds a touch above even, near 52 percent. We would not be surprised by anything from a bit above one in three to two in three. Anyone quoting a firm answer in either direction is selling something.

And note why this is a chance and not a verdict. Most of the money is not even spent yet. The build is still climbing, with its biggest years, 2027 through 2030, still ahead of us. So this is not a scorecard of something finished. It is a bet on a future that has barely begun. The honest output of a bet is a chance, not a yes or a no.

What the bet turns on is short to say. Whether one very large buyer stays afloat. Whether a grown-up industry keeps its margin. Whether demand keeps climbing past 2030 or levels off. None of those is decided yet. That is the whole of it.
