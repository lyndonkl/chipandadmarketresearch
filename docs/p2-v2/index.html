The US advertising market · 1833–2026

Who Holds the Yardstick

A history of the US advertising market, read through one question.

Every figure here comes from an outside source, and carries a grade – A, B, or C – for how solid that source is. We have not verified those sources ourselves. The grade tells you where a number came from and how firmly it is reported, not that we checked it against the world. The grades and intervals are derived from those sources. This research was assembled with AI, which can occasionally get something wrong. So if a figure matters to you, we strongly recommend cross-checking it against the source itself.

How to read this
a mark that holds steady = grade A – the number is in a primary record: a filing, a government dataset, a court finding.
a mark with a faint tremble = grade B – the number is reported by a credible source, one step off the primary record.
a mark that shimmers = grade C – the number is our own triangulation from several sources; the more it shimmers, the less certain it is.
cyan = an independent number – an audit, a census, a court finding: held by a third party, not the party being judged.
amber = a seller-owned number – the party being judged counting its own audience, setting its own price, billing its own log. The left spine carries the same amber → cyan → amber across the whole piece.
struck red = a corrected / DEAD claim – a popular legend this piece knocks down (contested claims show both values instead of a strike).
a left bracket marked A READING – the passage has crossed from the record into the author's synthesis.

Act I · The independent number is built

S0 · 2008 · A NUMBER THAT ISN'T THERE

A number that isn't there

A e6-measurement-006 ~18% YoY paid-click growth, Q4 2008 — no count, CPC, or query total disclosed. In its earnings release for the last quarter of 2008, Google told its shareholders one fact about its ads. People had clicked them about 18% more often than a year before. That was all. The company gave no total number of clicks. It gave no average price per click. It gave no count of searches.

Hold that gap in mind. It is the whole story of the American advertising market, read backward.

A reading

B e1-measurement-002 ABC founded 1914 in Chicago — the world's first circulation auditor. The 1914 anchor under an L5 reading. For about ninety years, from 1914 to the early 2000s, this market ran on an independently measured number. An outside body counted the audience. The price the agency charged to place the ads was a published figure. Before that stretch, and after it, the party that sold the ads owned the number itself. It counted its own readers and set its own price. The independent count was the exception, not the rule.

This is what I [Claude Opus 4.8] inferred from researching the advertising market over the last century. No single source in this material directly states who owns the published numbers or why the independent audit was the exception. But the pattern is hard to miss once you line the layers up. And while it is tempting to call the platform era [Google and the other digital ad platforms] a brand-new crisis, on this evidence it looks more like a return to the old ways.

B e1-measurement-002 ABC founded 1914 in Chicago — the world's first circulation auditor. A e6-measurement-006 2008 self-count set against the 1914 audit — the one load-bearing juxtaposition (the hero hands off here). Set 2008 against 1914. In 1914, in Chicago, publishers and advertisers merged two rival groups into the Audit Bureau of Circulations. It was the first body in the world to audit circulation – how many copies a newspaper actually sold. Before it, a publisher stated its own circulation, and the advertiser buying the space took the publisher's word for it. After it, an outsider checked the figure. The market had just built the outside audit – the very thing 2008 would quietly take back.

S1 · 1833–1914 · WHO KNEW THE PRICE

Who knew the price

Start at the price, because the price was once a public number.

64.9% advertising = 64.9% of US newspaper gross income, 1914 — $184.1M from advertisers against $99.5M from readers. (e1-sellers-003, A) B e1-events-001 Penny Sun, 1833: ~15,000 copies/day within ~2 years — the advertiser-funded model. The model is old. The New York Sun went on sale in 1833 at a penny a copy, and within about two years it was selling roughly 15,000 copies a day. You clearly couldn't run a newspaper on a penny a copy; something else had to be paying for it. By 1914, across all US newspapers, advertising supplied 64.9% of gross income – $184.1 million from advertisers against $99.5 million from readers. The advertising was subsidizing the paper.

To help with the buying and selling of advertisements, a new kind of business emerged: the advertising agency. It acted as a middleman between the advertiser and the newspaper. It bought space on the advertiser's behalf, and often wrote the ad as well.

B e2-creators-002 Standard 15% agency commission; on radio it covered production too. For that work it charged a commission of 15% of what the advertiser spent on space. That commission was the agency's price, and it was public. Everyone in the trade knew the rate. On radio, the same 15% did two jobs at once, because the sponsor's agency both bought the airtime and produced the program that ran on it. The 15% was only the visible part of the middleman's take. The rest, as the next section will show, stayed out of view.

A e4-pricing-001 The 15% commission shows up in later figures: census commissions-received held near 14% (14.13% 1977 → 13.18% 1992). s-AYER: no 1875 open-contract artifact in the corpus. This open pricing is usually traced to N. W. Ayer, a leading early ad agency, and its open contract, dated by tradition to 1875. Under an open contract the agency showed the advertiser exactly what it had paid the newspaper, so the advertiser could see the real cost of the space and the size of the agency's cut. Our own record does not hold that 1875 contract, so the founding document is missing here. The price itself, though, is not missing. The 15% commission shows up in later figures this piece relies on.

S2 · 1914 · WHO COUNTED THE READERS

Who counted the readers

Now the count. This is where the audit begins, and where the ninety-year exception begins with it.

B e1-measurement-002 ABC founded 1914 in Chicago — the world's first circulation auditor. The Audit Bureau of Circulations, founded in 1914, was a merger of a publisher-led body and an advertiser-led one. Its job was to correct a habit. Before it, a publisher simply stated its own circulation. The Bureau put an outsider between the seller's claim and the buyer's cheque. For the first time, the number that set the price did not belong to the party being paid.

S3 · 1944–1949 · THE COUNT CHANGES HANDS

The count changes hands

The independent number did not hold. It started drifting back almost as soon as it existed, and radio shows the drift.

~20% Hooper's vendor count ran ~20% above the co-op's, on the same telephone method after 1944 — same method, higher number. (e2-measurement-003, B; ci80 15–25) Two services measured the radio audience. After 1944 they used the same telephone method — calling homes to ask what was on. They still disagreed. The commercial vendor, C. E. Hooper, produced numbers that ran about 20% above the cooperative service's, on the same method. Analysts traced the gap to small technical choices: how rings were counted, how unknown programs were handled, how busy signals were treated. Same method, different number, and the vendor's was the higher one. This is a sequence, not a proven motive; the record shows the divergence, not a reason for it.

B e2-measurement-008 CBS guaranteed $3,000/wk per Hooper point Benny lost, Jan 1949 — the vendor rating written into a deal term. By the end of the decade the number had a price tag written into a contract. In January 1949, when Jack Benny moved from NBC to CBS, CBS guaranteed his sponsor $3,000 a week for every rating point Benny lost in the move. A rating was no longer a measurement off to the side. It was a term of the deal.

S4 · 1925–1938 · HOW MANY HANDS TOOK A CUT

How many hands took a cut

B e2-creators-002 The one disclosed plate — the published 15% commission. The 15% was never the whole cut. It was the one plate the trade published. Behind it stood a stack of hands, and most of them were not public.

73/27 the split the 1938 FCC audit EXTRACTED — NBC+CBS kept 73% ($32,046,218), paid 27% ($12,267,560) to 253 stations. A number wrested from the seller. (e2-sellers-002, A) The deepest split surfaced only because a regulator forced it into the open. In 1938 the FCC audited the networks. NBC and CBS together kept 73% of their network time sales — $32,046,218 — and paid the other 27%, $12,267,560, to the 253 stations that carried the programs. That 73/27 split was not a published rate. It was extracted by an audit.

A e2-pricing-002 AT&T sold network time at $2,600/hr, ~$750k/yr, end-1925 — scaffolding for the origin of the chain. C e3-pricing-003 Station reps took 5–20% on the net after the agency's cut — a range, no typical value; a hidden, unpublished plate. B e3-pricing-002 A 2% cash discount on the net, usually left out of take-rate comparisons. The chain had started earlier, and with the phone company. By the end of 1925, AT&T was selling time on its basic thirteen-station network at $2,600 an hour, taking in about $750,000 a year, before RCA bought the business. On top of the agency's 15%, a station representative firm took a further cut for selling local airtime to national buyers — a negotiated 5% to 20%, figured on what was left after the agency's share. The source gives that range and no typical figure, so it stays a range. And on top of that sat a 2% cash discount for paying the media quickly, a plate usually left out of any comparison.

DEAD The middleman's cut was a stable single 15%. It was three or four plates on different bases that cannot be added into one honest total — and the public one was the smallest.

So the middleman's cut was not a stable single 15%. It was three or four plates on different bases, and they cannot be added into one honest total. The public one was the smallest.

S5 · 1956–2003 · DID THE 15% DIE?

Did the 15% die?

The tidy version says DEADthe 15% commission died in the 1980s, killed by a court or by the internet. The record says otherwise, twice.

A e3-creators-001 AAAA consent decree, 1 Feb 1956 — ended collective 15% enforcement. It changed the law, not the price. B e3-creators-002 71% of the largest US advertisers still on commission in 1982 (ANA tracking). A e4-pricing-001 Census commissions-received held near 14%: 14.13% (1977), 13.92% (1982), 14.16% (1987), 13.18% (1992). The peak, not the death. A court did strike at it. A consent decree, entered on 1 February 1956, ended the trade associations' collective enforcement of the 15% and the "recognition" system that went with it. It changed the law. It did not change the price. As late as 1982, 71% of the largest US national advertisers were still paying their agencies by media commission. The census figure for commissions actually received held near 14% for years: 14.13% in 1977, 13.92% in 1982, 14.16% in 1987, 13.18% in 1992. The 15% did not die in this stretch. This was its peak.

DEAD Search killed the commission. The commission basis collapsed after the early-1990s unbundling of media buying from creative work — the corpus marks search here as a non-cause.

B e6-creators-002 Commission basis fell to ~10% by 2003 from 61% in 1994 — followed unbundling, not search. Its collapse came later, and not from search. The share of advertisers paying on a commission basis fell to about 10% by 2003, down from 61% in 1994. That collapse followed the unbundling of media buying from creative work in the early 1990s. It did not follow the arrival of search advertising. The corpus marks search here as a non-cause.

S6 · 1987 · THE INSTRUMENT, NOT THE AUDIENCE

The instrument, not the audience

In September 1987 the count changed again, and this time the change was in the ruler, not the world.

≈−10% Sept 1987 people meters measured ~10% fewer at network prime time — CBS/ABC each −13%, NBC −4%. The honest count cut the sellers' own numbers. (e4-measurement-001, B) Nielsen's people meters became the national television currency that month. They measured about 10% fewer people watching network prime time than the old method had. CBS and ABC each fell 13%. NBC fell 4%. No viewer got up off the couch. The audience did not shrink. The instrument that measured it changed, and the measured number dropped.

A reading

Note who got hurt. The more honest count cut the sellers' own figures. The networks were the sellers, and it was their numbers that fell. A market where the seller simply inflates its own count does not predict a seller adopting a ruler that shrinks its numbers. This is one of several moments where the honest number cost whoever was being measured — a fact the tidy "sellers rig everything" story cannot hold.

S7 · 1914–2008 · THE BUYER'S OWN METER

The buyer's own meter

One buyer never trusted the seller's audience count and never had to. That is the direct-response buyer, and its meter is the oldest independent number in the market.

C e1-buyers-008 Direct-response pool ~$192M in 1914, ~16% of the benchmark — wide, low-confidence. B e3-buyers-001 Direct mail = 24% of US national advertising in 1956, more than broadcast. The direct-response buyer pays only for a measured reply. Mail-order houses and patent-medicine sellers keyed their coupons and counted what came back. In 1914 this pool was already large — very roughly $192 million, about a sixth of the benchmark total. That early figure is a wide, low-confidence estimate, third-hand, and it should be read as a range, not a point. By 1956 direct mail alone was 24% of all US national advertising, a larger share than broadcast. Performance advertising was not a platform invention. It was the largest single national medium for about half a century before platforms existed.

DEAD Platforms invented performance advertising. The keyed-reply buyer was the largest single national medium for ~half a century before platforms.

A e6-buyers-008 57% of US internet ad revenue bought pay-per-action in 2008 (up from 51%); 39% CPM. On the internet, this buyer's model won the medium. By 2008, 57% of US internet ad revenue was bought on a pay-per-action basis, up from 51% a year earlier. Only 39% was bought by the impression. The buyer's independent meter finally took over — and it took over in the one medium where the seller also owns the count of the actions being paid for. The most-wanted independent number landed back inside the seller's log.

S9 · 1975–2025 · THE DIAL

The dial

Targeting got extraordinarily fine, and fineness turns out not to be the point.

A reading

S10 · 2002–2024 · THE GOVERNED PRICE

The governed price

The clearing price came dressed as a neutral machine. It was not neutral. The seller set it.

A mech-adwords-001 AdWords Select, 20 Feb 2002: advertisers "pay the lowest amount possible". AdWords Select launched on 20 February 2002 with a promise: advertisers would "pay the lowest amount possible." The auction ranked ads by bid and by click-through rate, then discounted the winner toward the runner-up. It read like a fair machine. But the machine ran on a log the seller owned, recorded, and billed.

A e5-pricing-004 Overture was pure first-price, ~$0.23/click, listings needed editorial approval. The founding auction was not even that machine. Overture's, in late 2001, was pure first-price — ads ranked in descending order of bid, each advertiser paying its own bid, averaging about $0.23 a click. The "neutral" second-price ideal was a later, Google-era design, not an original state of grace. Overture's door was also gated: its listings needed human editorial approval. AdWords let the buyer in without one.

A mech-rgsp-001 rGSP (2019): +5.91% top-slot CPC on PC/tablet, +4.85% mobile; advertisers cannot opt out. A mech-knobs-001 Three pricing knobs — 'Butternut Squash', format pricing, rGSP — 'intentional pricing'; exponent never disclosed. A mech-mehta-004 Court: Google tested raising text-ad prices 5%+ — "the results have been largely consistent — it can". Then the seller tuned the price. In 2019, Google changed its search auction — the design the court later called rGSP — and its own pre-launch tests raised the top-slot cost per click by 5.91% on PCs and tablets and 4.85% on mobile. Advertisers could not opt out. In 2024, a federal court, in its findings of fact, named three pricing controls on the search text-ad auction — one called internally "Butternut Squash," plus format pricing and rGSP — as "intentional pricing." The squashing setting, the court noted, has never been publicly disclosed. The same court found that Google had tested whether it could raise text-ad prices by 5% or more without losing many advertisers, and wrote: "the results have been largely consistent — it can."

A mech-tac-003 Syndicated take rate ~9% (2002) → 24.7% (2006) → 21.3% (2008) — the ratchet reversed. One fact cuts against reading this as pure extraction. The cut does not only ratchet upward. On Google's syndicated inventory, the take rate rose from about 9% in 2002 to a peak of 24.7% in 2006 — and then fell to 21.3% by 2008, as competition for partners intensified. A pure maximiser would not let its own rate fall.

S12 · 1919–2025 · EVERY NEW UNIT IS BORN UNCOUNTED

Every new unit is born uncounted

A reading

Here is the engine underneath all of it, and it is structural, not a scheme. Every new carrier ships a new priced unit that no outside body can yet count. So the seller counts it first. This is my reading of the mechanism; it is adjacency in the record, not a stated law.

At the end it understates internet advertising by about half — $10,529 million for 2007, against the industry's own $21,206 million — and then it simply stops, at 2007.
