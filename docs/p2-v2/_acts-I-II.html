S0 · 2008 · A NUMBER THAT ISN'T THERE

A number that isn't there

A e6-measurement-006 ~18% YoY paid-click growth, Q4 2008 — no count, CPC, or query total disclosed. In its earnings release for the last quarter of 2008, Google told its shareholders one fact about its ads. People had clicked them about 18% more often than a year before. That was all. The company gave no total number of clicks. It gave no average price per click. It gave no count of searches. The growth was disclosed. The count was not.

Hold that gap in mind. It is the whole story of the American advertising market, read backward.

A reading

B e1-measurement-002 ABC founded 1914 in Chicago — the world's first circulation auditor. The 1914 anchor under an L5 reading. For about ninety years, from 1914 to the early 2000s, this market ran on an independent number. An outside body counted the audience. The price of the middleman's work was a published figure. Before that stretch, and again after it, the party being judged owned the number instead. The seller counted its own readers. The seller set its own price. The independent count was the exception, not the rule.

That reading is mine, drawn from the record that follows. No single source in this material states it. But the pattern is hard to miss once you line the layers up. It is tempting to call the platform era a brand-new crisis. On this evidence it looks more like a return.

B e1-measurement-002 ABC founded 1914 in Chicago — the world's first circulation auditor. A e6-measurement-006 2008 self-count set against the 1914 audit — the one load-bearing juxtaposition (the hero hands off here). Set 2008 against 1914. In 1914, in Chicago, publishers and advertisers merged two rival groups into the Audit Bureau of Circulations. It was the first body in the world to audit circulation. Before it, a publisher stated its own sales and the buyer took the publisher's word. After it, an outsider checked the figure. The market had just built the thing that 2008 would quietly take back.

Start at the price, because the price was once a public number.

S2 · 1914 · WHO COUNTED THE READERS

Who counted the readers

Now the count. This is where the audit begins, and where the ninety-year exception begins with it.

B e1-measurement-002 ABC founded 1914 in Chicago — the world's first circulation auditor. The Audit Bureau of Circulations, founded in 1914, was a merger of a publisher-led body and an advertiser-led one. Its job was to correct a habit. Before it, a publisher simply stated its own circulation. The Bureau put an outsider between the seller's claim and the buyer's cheque. For the first time, the number that set the price did not belong to the party being paid.

A reading

B e1-measurement-002 1914 built an independent count. A e6-measurement-006 2008 reports a growth rate and keeps the count to itself — the s2↔s0 juxtaposition. Read against the opener, the shape of the whole piece is already visible. The market spent 1914 building an independent count. Ninety years later it would report a growth rate and keep the count to itself.

S3 · 1944–1949 · THE COUNT CHANGES HANDS

The count changes hands

The independent number did not hold. It started drifting back almost as soon as it existed, and radio shows the drift.

~20% Hooper's vendor count ran ~20% above the co-op's, on the same telephone method after 1944 — same method, higher number. (e2-measurement-003, B; ci80 15–25) Two services measured the radio audience. After 1944 they used the same telephone method — calling homes to ask what was on. They still disagreed. The commercial vendor, C. E. Hooper, produced numbers that ran about 20% above the cooperative service's, on the same method. Analysts traced the gap to small technical choices: how rings were counted, how unknown programs were handled, how busy signals were treated. Same method, different number, and the vendor's was the higher one. This is a sequence, not a proven motive; the record shows the divergence, not a reason for it.

B e2-measurement-008 CBS guaranteed $3,000/wk per Hooper point Benny lost, Jan 1949 — the vendor rating written into a deal term. By the end of the decade the number had a price tag written into a contract. In January 1949, when Jack Benny moved from NBC to CBS, CBS guaranteed his sponsor $3,000 a week for every rating point Benny lost in the move. A rating was no longer a measurement off to the side. It was a term of the deal.

S4 · 1925–1938 · HOW MANY HANDS TOOK A CUT

How many hands took a cut

B e2-creators-002 The one disclosed plate — the published 15% commission. The 15% was never the whole cut. It was the one plate the trade published. Behind it stood a stack of hands, and most of them were not public.

73/27 the split the 1938 FCC audit EXTRACTED — NBC+CBS kept 73% ($32,046,218), paid 27% ($12,267,560) to 253 stations. A number wrested from the seller. (e2-sellers-002, A) The deepest split surfaced only because a regulator forced it into the open. In 1938 the FCC audited the networks. NBC and CBS together kept 73% of their network time sales — $32,046,218 — and paid the other 27%, $12,267,560, to the 253 stations that carried the programs. That 73/27 split was not a published rate. It was extracted by an audit.

A e2-pricing-002 AT&T sold network time at $2,600/hr, ~$750k/yr, end-1925 — scaffolding for the origin of the chain. C e3-pricing-003 Station reps took 5–20% on the net after the agency's cut — a range, no typical value; a hidden, unpublished plate. B e3-pricing-002 A 2% cash discount on the net, usually left out of take-rate comparisons. The chain had started earlier, and with the phone company. By the end of 1925, AT&T was selling time on its basic thirteen-station network at $2,600 an hour, taking in about $750,000 a year, before RCA bought the business. On top of the agency's 15%, a station representative firm took a further cut for selling local airtime to national buyers — a negotiated 5% to 20%, figured on what was left after the agency's share. The source gives that range and no typical figure, so it stays a range. And on top of that sat a 2% cash discount for paying the media quickly, a plate usually left out of any comparison.

So the middleman's cut was not a stable single 15%. It was three or four plates on different bases, and they cannot be added into one honest total. The public one was the smallest.

S5 · 1956–2003 · DID THE 15% DIE?

Did the 15% die?

The tidy version says DEADthe 15% commission died in the 1980s, killed by a court or by the internet. The record says otherwise, twice.

A e3-creators-001 AAAA consent decree, 1 Feb 1956 — ended collective 15% enforcement. It changed the law, not the price. B e3-creators-002 71% of the largest US advertisers still on commission in 1982 (ANA tracking). A e4-pricing-001 Census commissions-received held near 14%: 14.13% (1977), 13.92% (1982), 14.16% (1987), 13.18% (1992). The peak, not the death. A court did strike at it. A consent decree, entered on 1 February 1956, ended the trade associations' collective enforcement of the 15% and the "recognition" system that went with it. It changed the law. It did not change the price. As late as 1982, 71% of the largest US national advertisers were still paying their agencies by media commission. The census figure for commissions actually received held near 14% for years: 14.13% in 1977, 13.92% in 1982, 14.16% in 1987, 13.18% in 1992. The 15% did not die in this stretch. This was its peak.

DEAD Search killed the commission. The commission basis collapsed after the early-1990s unbundling of media buying from creative work — the corpus marks search here as a non-cause.

B e6-creators-002 Commission basis fell to ~10% by 2003 from 61% in 1994 — followed unbundling, not search. Its collapse came later, and not from search. The share of advertisers paying on a commission basis fell to about 10% by 2003, down from 61% in 1994. That collapse followed the unbundling of media buying from creative work in the early 1990s. It did not follow the arrival of search advertising. The corpus marks search here as a non-cause.

S6 · 1987 · THE INSTRUMENT, NOT THE AUDIENCE

The instrument, not the audience

In September 1987 the count changed again, and this time the change was in the ruler, not the world.

≈−10% Sept 1987 people meters measured ~10% fewer at network prime time — CBS/ABC each −13%, NBC −4%. The honest count cut the sellers' own numbers. (e4-measurement-001, B) Nielsen's people meters became the national television currency that month. They measured about 10% fewer people watching network prime time than the old method had. CBS and ABC each fell 13%. NBC fell 4%. No viewer got up off the couch. The audience did not shrink. The instrument that measured it changed, and the measured number dropped.

A reading

Note who got hurt. The more honest count cut the sellers' own figures. The networks were the sellers, and it was their numbers that fell. A market where the seller simply inflates its own count does not predict a seller adopting a ruler that shrinks its numbers. This is one of several moments where the honest number cost whoever was being measured — a fact the tidy "sellers rig everything" story cannot hold.
