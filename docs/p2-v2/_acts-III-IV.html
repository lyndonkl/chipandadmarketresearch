S7 · 1914–2008 · THE BUYER'S OWN METER

The buyer's own meter

One buyer never trusted the seller's audience count and never had to. That is the direct-response buyer, and its meter is the oldest independent number in the market.

C e1-buyers-008 Direct-response pool ~$192M in 1914, ~16% of the benchmark — wide, low-confidence. B e3-buyers-001 Direct mail = 24% of US national advertising in 1956, more than broadcast. The direct-response buyer pays only for a measured reply. Mail-order houses and patent-medicine sellers keyed their coupons and counted what came back. In 1914 this pool was already large — very roughly $192 million, about a sixth of the benchmark total. That early figure is a wide, low-confidence estimate, third-hand, and it should be read as a range, not a point. By 1956 direct mail alone was 24% of all US national advertising, a larger share than broadcast. Performance advertising was not a platform invention. It was the largest single national medium for about half a century before platforms existed.

DEAD Platforms invented performance advertising. The keyed-reply buyer was the largest single national medium for ~half a century before platforms.

A e6-buyers-008 57% of US internet ad revenue bought pay-per-action in 2008 (up from 51%); 39% CPM. On the internet, this buyer's model won the medium. By 2008, 57% of US internet ad revenue was bought on a pay-per-action basis, up from 51% a year earlier. Only 39% was bought by the impression. The buyer's independent meter finally took over — and it took over in the one medium where the seller also owns the count of the actions being paid for. The most-wanted independent number landed back inside the seller's log.

S8 · 1914–1971 · WHO GETS IN THE DOOR

Who gets in the door

Two questions get merged here that should stay apart. Who is allowed to buy? And who takes a cut at the door?

C e1-buyers-007 1914 newspaper classified carried no agency commission and no cost of preparation. The open, commission-free door is old. In 1914, newspaper classified advertising carried no agency commission and no cost of preparation. A small buyer could place an ad with no middleman a hundred years ago. Self-serve buying is not a platform-era invention.

DEAD Self-serve buying is a platform-era invention. 1914 newspaper classified carried no agency commission and no cost of preparation.

61.4→66.3% national advertisers' share of US ad spending, 2002→2007 — the money leaned toward national buyers: a long tail of heads, not of dollars. (e6-buyers-005, B) And the money did not democratise the way the story says. "Search opened the market to the little guy and moved spending to a long tail" — the dollars run the other way. National advertisers' share of US ad spending rose from 61.4% in 2002 to 66.3% in 2007. More heads got in the door. The money leaned harder toward national buyers, not away from them. It was a long tail of heads, not of dollars.

DEAD Search democratised money and moved spending to a long tail of dollars. National share rose 61.4%→66.3% (2002–07): a long tail of heads, not of dollars.

B e3-buyers-004 Cigarette makers paid broadcasters ~$225M/yr before the 2 Jan 1971 ban. B e3-medium-011 1971 was the era's only down year for US TV advertising, −1.7%. The state works the door too, and it can expel a whole class of buyer. Cigarette makers were paying broadcasters about $225 million a year for airtime before the ban that took effect on 2 January 1971. That year was the only one of its era in which US television advertising fell — down 1.7%. The state is the bouncer, and the bouncer sometimes throws a paying customer out.

S9 · 1975–2025 · THE DIAL

The dial

Targeting got extraordinarily fine, and fineness turns out not to be the point.

A reading

A e3-targeting-005 Direct mail addressed 71.1M US households in 1975 — ~279,000× finer than a TV spot. B e4-targeting-004 A 2% reply on a rented list was a successful campaign (rate on pieces mailed). B e5-targeting-005 Read-vs-mailed guard: Overture's implied 15.9% reply is on pieces read, not mailed. By 1975, direct mail could address 71.1 million US households one at a time — a granularity roughly 279,000 times finer than the average unit spot on television. Fine did not mean answered. In that era, a 2% reply on a rented list was a successful campaign. Ninety-eight of a hundred recipients ignored it. That 2% is a reply among pieces mailed; it must not be set beside search's reply rates, which the era's filings measured among pieces actually read. The record carries a reply rate for only one era, so I cannot claim the yield stayed flat across the century. It only shows the one figure.

442M Overture paid clicks, Q4 2001 — matched to typed words, "with no cookie and no profile," across ~53,000 advertisers. (e5-targeting-004, A) Then search changed the axis. In the last quarter of 2001, Overture delivered 442 million paid clicks matched to the words people typed — "with no cookie and no profile" — across about 53,000 advertisers. The most powerful new targeting of the age carried intent without identity. Advertising did not march to the individual. It marched to the countable unit, and the most countable unit was a typed query attached to no one in particular.

DEAD Advertising marched to the individual. It marched to the countable unit — a typed query attached to no one in particular.

C e7-buyers-003 Vendor survey: 71% ran Performance Max in 2025 (up from 60%) — soft endpoint, wide band. B e7-targeting-002 Apple ATT opt-in ~16% at launch (May 2021) → ~25% by mid-2022. Later the buyer handed the dial back to the machine. By 2025, a vendor survey of more than 170 advertisers found 71% running Google's automated Performance Max campaigns, up from 60% the year before. That figure is a vendor panel, not a count of Google's whole advertiser base, and it carries a wide band; it is a soft endpoint, not the point of the story. And the state can prise identity back off the ad: Apple's App Tracking Transparency drew opt-in from about 16% of users at its launch in May 2021, rising to roughly 25% by mid-2022.

S10 · 2002–2024 · THE GOVERNED PRICE

The governed price

The clearing price came dressed as a neutral machine. It was not neutral. The seller set it.

A mech-adwords-001 AdWords Select, 20 Feb 2002: advertisers "pay the lowest amount possible". AdWords Select launched on 20 February 2002 with a promise: advertisers would "pay the lowest amount possible." The auction ranked ads by bid and by click-through rate, then discounted the winner toward the runner-up. It read like a fair machine. But the machine ran on a log the seller owned, recorded, and billed.

A e5-pricing-004 Overture was pure first-price, ~$0.23/click, listings needed editorial approval. The founding auction was not even that machine. Overture's, in late 2001, was pure first-price — ads ranked in descending order of bid, each advertiser paying its own bid, averaging about $0.23 a click. The "neutral" second-price ideal was a later, Google-era design, not an original state of grace. Overture's door was also gated: its listings needed human editorial approval. AdWords let the buyer in without one.

A mech-rgsp-001 rGSP (2019): +5.91% top-slot CPC on PC/tablet, +4.85% mobile; advertisers cannot opt out. A mech-knobs-001 Three pricing knobs — 'Butternut Squash', format pricing, rGSP — 'intentional pricing'; exponent never disclosed. A mech-mehta-004 Court: Google tested raising text-ad prices 5%+ — "the results have been largely consistent — it can". Then the seller tuned the price. In 2019, Google changed its search auction — the design the court later called rGSP — and its own pre-launch tests raised the top-slot cost per click by 5.91% on PCs and tablets and 4.85% on mobile. Advertisers could not opt out. In 2024, a federal court, in its findings of fact, named three pricing controls on the search text-ad auction — one called internally "Butternut Squash," plus format pricing and rGSP — as "intentional pricing." The squashing setting, the court noted, has never been publicly disclosed. The same court found that Google had tested whether it could raise text-ad prices by 5% or more without losing many advertisers, and wrote: "the results have been largely consistent — it can."

A mech-tac-003 Syndicated take rate ~9% (2002) → 24.7% (2006) → 21.3% (2008) — the ratchet reversed. One fact cuts against reading this as pure extraction. The cut does not only ratchet upward. On Google's syndicated inventory, the take rate rose from about 9% in 2002 to a peak of 24.7% in 2006 — and then fell to 21.3% by 2008, as competition for partners intensified. A pure maximiser would not let its own rate fall.

S11 · 1943–2024 · THE STATE MOVES THE TOP OF THE STACK

The state moves the top of the stack

Concentration in this market is built with capital. The state moves it around and caps it, and only rarely breaks it.

A e6-sellers-001 Overture led paid search: $667.7M revenue 2002 vs Google's $439.5M total. The capital point is plain in the numbers. Overture led the paid-search business on revenue entering the 2000s — $667.7 million in 2002, against Google's $439.5 million in total revenue. The leader had the bigger business, and the corpus does not credit Google with inventing the per-click auction. Overture led it and built it.

DEAD Google invented search advertising. Overture led the paid-search business on revenue and built the per-click auction; the corpus does not credit Google with inventing it.

B e2-events-006 Edward J. Noble bought NBC's Blue Network for $8M, 12 Oct 1943 — a sale, not a build. A e3-sellers-002 From 1954 the FCC capped owners at 7 TV stations, max 5 VHF. B e4-sellers-004 RBOC Yellow Pages sold $9,517M of advertising in 1993 — more than radio or magazines. The state's record is relocation and ceilings. In 1943 a third national network came into being by sale, not by construction. Edward J. Noble bought NBC's Blue Network for $8 million on 12 October 1943. An incumbent's spare network changed hands; a rival did not build one. (What forced that sale is not in this record, so I state only the sale.) From 1954 the FCC capped any single owner at seven television stations, no more than five of them VHF — a standing ceiling. After 1984 the regional Bell companies became the main publishers of the Yellow Pages. They sold $9,517 million of advertising in 1993, more than radio or magazines: the state minting fresh sellers.

A e6-targeting-004 Targeting entered by purchase: DoubleClick $3.1bn (Apr 2007), aQuantive ~$6bn, Right Media $680M, 24/7 $649M — largest deals of the year. Audience targeting itself entered by purchase, not by build. Google agreed to buy DoubleClick for $3.1 billion in April 2007. Microsoft bought aQuantive for about $6 billion. Yahoo bought Right Media for $680 million. WPP bought 24/7 Real Media for $649 million. Those were the largest such deals of that year, not typical ones.

B e2-sellers-006 Postwar boom tripled the AM dial: ~900 stations (1945) → ~2,500 (early 1950s). One deconcentration was real, and the state licensed it without designing it: a postwar boom roughly tripled the AM dial, from about 900 stations in 1945 to roughly 2,500 by the early 1950s.

$26.3B search default revenue share Google paid in 2021 — the court found it foreclosed competition, then left the machine intact. (e7-events-009, A) A mech-mehta-004 The court called Google's search text-ad prices "supracompetitive" — a seller-owned number surfaced by a court finding. And when the state named the modern structure unlawful, it did so without pulling it apart. In 2024 a court found that Google had paid $26.3 billion in search default revenue share in 2021. It ruled that spending had foreclosed competition. It also found that Google charged prices above competitive levels for search text ads — "supracompetitive," in the court's word. The finding named the conduct. It did not dismantle the machine.

S12 · 1919–2025 · EVERY NEW UNIT IS BORN UNCOUNTED

Every new unit is born uncounted

A reading

Here is the engine underneath all of it, and it is structural, not a scheme. Every new carrier ships a new priced unit that no outside body can yet count. So the seller counts it first. This is my reading of the mechanism; it is adjacency in the record, not a stated law.

A e6-medium-006 Coen series understates internet by ~half ($10,529M vs $21,206M, 2007) and stops at 2007. The clearest evidence is the historian's own ruler going blind. The long Coen/McCann series tracked US advertising spending from 1919. At the end it understates internet advertising by about half — $10,529 million for 2007, against the industry's own $21,206 million — and then it simply stops, at 2007. The standard measure went dark exactly as the new unit arrived.

B e2-medium-006 TV entered the series in 1949 at $58M, 1.1% of total. B e4-medium-002 Total TV first passed newspapers in 1992 — $31,079M vs $30,737M. A e7-medium-001 US internet ad revenue $23,448M (2008) → $294,593M (2025), 12.6×; only down year 2009. A e6-scale-009 Help-wanted classified fell 91% from its 2000 peak; real-estate classified fell with housing, not search. The medium-conquest story fails on the same data. Television entered the series in 1949 at $58 million, 1.1% of the total. Total television did not pass newspapers until 1992, and then by about 1% — $31,079 million against $30,737 million. Shares moved slowly. No medium killed the last one in a single stroke. Internet advertising did grow fast once someone counted it: from $23,448 million in 2008 to $294,593 million in 2025, about twelve and a half times, with a single down year in 2009. And "the internet killed classified" is too simple. Newspaper help-wanted classified did collapse — down 91% from its 2000 peak. But real-estate classified peaked in 2006 and fell with the housing market, not with search.

DEAD Each medium killed the last · TV conquered advertising · the internet killed classified (single cause). Shares moved slowly; no medium killed the last in one stroke; help-wanted classified collapsed but real-estate classified fell with the housing market, not search.

S13 · 1922–2025 · WHICH TOTAL?

Which total?

Every "share of advertising" is a claim about a basis. It is not a plain fact, and direct mail is the tell.

B e2-medium-004 Direct mail: 14.5% of US ad spend in 1949, peak 16.8% in 1938. B e7-scale-001 2025 US total ~$405B on media-owner basis (MAGNA $398B) — excludes direct mail entirely. Direct mail was the second-largest named medium in most years the data splits by medium — 14.5% of spending in 1949, and a peak of 16.8% in 1938. Yet the 2025 headline total leaves it out. US advertising in 2025 ran about $405 billion on the industry's media-owner-revenue basis — MAGNA's figure is $398 billion — and that basis excludes direct mail entirely. The biggest old channel is written out of the new total.

C e7-scale-006 National-brand pool ~$145.7B, 36.6% of $398B; IRS $473B vs MAGNA $325B (2022) = $148B rail wedge. The rails disagree by more than the fine print admits. On the same media-owner basis, the "national brand" pool for 2025 is about $145.7 billion, 36.6% of MAGNA's $398 billion — the largest or joint-largest pool, on a wide, low-confidence estimate. Change the rail and the whole picture moves: the IRS recorded $473 billion of corporate advertising deductions for 2022, against MAGNA's $325 billion for the same year. That is a $148 billion gap between two counts of the same thing. Any chart of this has to name its rail on the axis, not in a footnote. There is no single US advertising total waiting to be found.

DEAD There is a single US ad-market total waiting to be found. Change the rail and the whole picture moves; every share is a claim about a basis.

B e2-scale-004 Advertising peaked at 3.0% of US GDP in 1922. B e2-scale-005 Advertising bottomed at 1.2% of US GDP in 1944. C e7-scale-002 ~1.32% of US GDP in 2025 — different basis, not commensurable with the old series. Intensity over time tells the same cautionary story. On the long Coen series, advertising peaked at 3.0% of GDP in 1922 and bottomed at 1.2% in 1944. A 2025 reading puts it near 1.32% of GDP. But that last number sits on a different basis and cannot be lined up against the old series; the fall is partly real and partly a change in what counts as advertising at all.

Back to the hole

The count is still a hole, and the hole is where we started.

>$26B the IAB's own estimate of misallocated US media spend a better count could unlock in a year or two — recoverable, not settled. (e7-measurement-005, B) The industry knows it. That is an estimate of what might be recovered, not a settled figure.
