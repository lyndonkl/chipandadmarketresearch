# Who Holds the Yardstick

*A history of the US advertising market, read through one question.*

---

## A number that isn't there

In its earnings release for the last quarter of 2008, Google told its shareholders one fact about its ads. People had clicked them about 18% more often than a year before. That was all. The company gave no total number of clicks. It gave no average price per click. It gave no count of searches.

Hold that gap in mind. It is the whole story of the American advertising market, read backward.

For about ninety years, from 1914 to the early 2000s, this market ran on an independently measured number. An outside body counted the audience. The price the agency charged to place the ads was a published figure. Before that stretch, and after it, the party that sold the ads owned the number itself. It counted its own readers and set its own price. The independent count was the exception, not the rule.

This is what I [Claude Opus 4.8] inferred from researching the advertising market over the last century. No single source in this material directly states who owns the published numbers or why the independent audit was the exception. But the pattern is hard to miss once you line the layers up. And while it is tempting to call the platform era [Google and the other digital ad platforms] a brand-new crisis, on this evidence it looks more like a return to the old ways.

Set 2008 against 1914. In 1914, in Chicago, publishers and advertisers merged two rival groups into the Audit Bureau of Circulations. It was the first body in the world to audit circulation – how many copies a newspaper actually sold. Before it, a publisher stated its own circulation, and the advertiser buying the space took the publisher's word for it. After it, an outsider checked the figure. The market had just built the outside audit – the very thing 2008 would quietly take back.

## Who knew the price

Start at the price, because the price was once a public number.

The model is old. The New York Sun went on sale in 1833 at a penny a copy, and within about two years it was selling roughly 15,000 copies a day. You clearly couldn't run a newspaper on a penny a copy; something else had to be paying for it. By 1914, across all US newspapers, advertising supplied 64.9% of gross income – $184.1 million from advertisers against $99.5 million from readers. The advertising was subsidizing the paper.

For the buyer who did not want to run a paper, there was an agency, and the agency's price was disclosed. The standard commission held at 15% of what the advertiser spent on media. On radio that one number covered two jobs at once: buying the airtime and making the program. Fifteen percent, stated openly, was the visible top plate of the middleman's cut.

The discipline is usually dated to N. W. Ayer's open contract of 1875, which is said to have shown the client what the agency paid the publisher. No copy of that contract survives in this record. The disclosed price is real in the figures that come later. Its founding document is missing here, and I will not invent one.

## Who counted the readers

Now the count. This is where the audit begins, and where the ninety-year exception begins with it.

The Audit Bureau of Circulations, founded in 1914, was a merger of a publisher-led body and an advertiser-led one. Its job was to correct a habit. Before it, a publisher simply stated its own circulation. The Bureau put an outsider between the seller's claim and the buyer's cheque. For the first time, the number that set the price did not belong to the party being paid.

Read against the opener, the shape of the whole piece is already visible. The market spent 1914 building an independent count. Ninety years later it would report a growth rate and keep the count to itself.

## The count changes hands

The independent number did not hold. It started drifting back almost as soon as it existed, and radio shows the drift.

Two services measured the radio audience. After 1944 they used the same telephone method — calling homes to ask what was on. They still disagreed. The commercial vendor, C. E. Hooper, produced numbers that ran about 20% above the cooperative service's, on the same method. Analysts traced the gap to small technical choices: how rings were counted, how unknown programs were handled, how busy signals were treated. Same method, different number, and the vendor's was the higher one. This is a sequence, not a proven motive; the record shows the divergence, not a reason for it.

By the end of the decade the number had a price tag written into a contract. In January 1949, when Jack Benny moved from NBC to CBS, CBS guaranteed his sponsor $3,000 a week for every rating point Benny lost in the move. A rating was no longer a measurement off to the side. It was a term of the deal.

## How many hands took a cut

The 15% was never the whole cut. It was the one plate the trade published. Behind it stood a stack of hands, and most of them were not public.

The deepest split surfaced only because a regulator forced it into the open. In 1938 the FCC audited the networks. NBC and CBS together kept 73% of their network time sales — $32,046,218 — and paid the other 27%, $12,267,560, to the 253 stations that carried the programs. That 73/27 split was not a published rate. It was extracted by an audit.

The chain had started earlier, and with the phone company. By the end of 1925, AT&T was selling time on its basic thirteen-station network at $2,600 an hour, taking in about $750,000 a year, before RCA bought the business. On top of the agency's 15%, a station representative firm took a further cut for selling local airtime to national buyers — a negotiated 5% to 20%, figured on what was left after the agency's share. The source gives that range and no typical figure, so it stays a range. And on top of that sat a 2% cash discount for paying the media quickly, a plate usually left out of any comparison.

So the middleman's cut was not a stable single 15%. It was three or four plates on different bases, and they cannot be added into one honest total. The public one was the smallest.

## Did the 15% die?

The tidy version says the 15% commission died in the 1980s, killed by a court or by the internet. The record says otherwise, twice.

A court did strike at it. A consent decree, entered on 1 February 1956, ended the trade associations' collective enforcement of the 15% and the "recognition" system that went with it. It changed the law. It did not change the price. As late as 1982, 71% of the largest US national advertisers were still paying their agencies by media commission. The census figure for commissions actually received held near 14% for years: 14.13% in 1977, 13.92% in 1982, 14.16% in 1987, 13.18% in 1992. The 15% did not die in this stretch. This was its peak.

Its collapse came later, and not from search. The share of advertisers paying on a commission basis fell to about 10% by 2003, down from 61% in 1994. That collapse followed the unbundling of media buying from creative work in the early 1990s. It did not follow the arrival of search advertising. The corpus marks search here as a non-cause.

## The instrument, not the audience

In September 1987 the count changed again, and this time the change was in the ruler, not the world.

Nielsen's people meters became the national television currency that month. They measured about 10% fewer people watching network prime time than the old method had. CBS and ABC each fell 13%. NBC fell 4%. No viewer got up off the couch. The audience did not shrink. The instrument that measured it changed, and the measured number dropped.

Note who got hurt. The more honest count cut the sellers' own figures. The networks were the sellers, and it was their numbers that fell. A market where the seller simply inflates its own count does not predict a seller adopting a ruler that shrinks its numbers. This is one of several moments where the honest number cost whoever was being measured — a fact the tidy "sellers rig everything" story cannot hold.

## The buyer's own meter

One buyer never trusted the seller's audience count and never had to. That is the direct-response buyer, and its meter is the oldest independent number in the market.

The direct-response buyer pays only for a measured reply. Mail-order houses and patent-medicine sellers keyed their coupons and counted what came back. In 1914 this pool was already large — very roughly $192 million, about a sixth of the benchmark total. That early figure is a wide, low-confidence estimate, third-hand, and it should be read as a range, not a point. By 1956 direct mail alone was 24% of all US national advertising, a larger share than broadcast. Performance advertising was not a platform invention. It was the largest single national medium for about half a century before platforms existed.

On the internet, this buyer's model won the medium. By 2008, 57% of US internet ad revenue was bought on a pay-per-action basis, up from 51% a year earlier. Only 39% was bought by the impression. The buyer's independent meter finally took over — and it took over in the one medium where the seller also owns the count of the actions being paid for. The most-wanted independent number landed back inside the seller's log.

## Who gets in the door

Two questions get merged here that should stay apart. Who is allowed to buy? And who takes a cut at the door?

The open, commission-free door is old. In 1914, newspaper classified advertising carried no agency commission and no cost of preparation. A small buyer could place an ad with no middleman a hundred years ago. Self-serve buying is not a platform-era invention.

And the money did not democratise the way the story says. "Search opened the market to the little guy and moved spending to a long tail" — the dollars run the other way. National advertisers' share of US ad spending rose from 61.4% in 2002 to 66.3% in 2007. More heads got in the door. The money leaned harder toward national buyers, not away from them. It was a long tail of heads, not of dollars.

The state works the door too, and it can expel a whole class of buyer. Cigarette makers were paying broadcasters about $225 million a year for airtime before the ban that took effect on 2 January 1971. That year was the only one of its era in which US television advertising fell — down 1.7%. The state is the bouncer, and the bouncer sometimes throws a paying customer out.

## The dial

Targeting got extraordinarily fine, and fineness turns out not to be the point.

By 1975, direct mail could address 71.1 million US households one at a time — a granularity roughly 279,000 times finer than the average unit spot on television. Fine did not mean answered. In that era, a 2% reply on a rented list was a successful campaign. Ninety-eight of a hundred recipients ignored it. That 2% is a reply among pieces *mailed*; it must not be set beside search's reply rates, which the era's filings measured among pieces actually *read*. The record carries a reply rate for only one era, so I cannot claim the yield stayed flat across the century. It only shows the one figure.

Then search changed the axis. In the last quarter of 2001, Overture delivered 442 million paid clicks matched to the words people typed — "with no cookie and no profile" — across about 53,000 advertisers. The most powerful new targeting of the age carried intent without identity. Advertising did not march to the individual. It marched to the countable unit, and the most countable unit was a typed query attached to no one in particular.

Later the buyer handed the dial back to the machine. By 2025, a vendor survey of more than 170 advertisers found 71% running Google's automated Performance Max campaigns, up from 60% the year before. That figure is a vendor panel, not a count of Google's whole advertiser base, and it carries a wide band; it is a soft endpoint, not the point of the story. And the state can prise identity back off the ad: Apple's App Tracking Transparency drew opt-in from about 16% of users at its launch in May 2021, rising to roughly 25% by mid-2022.

## The governed price

The clearing price came dressed as a neutral machine. It was not neutral. The seller set it.

AdWords Select launched on 20 February 2002 with a promise: advertisers would "pay the lowest amount possible." The auction ranked ads by bid and by click-through rate, then discounted the winner toward the runner-up. It read like a fair machine. But the machine ran on a log the seller owned, recorded, and billed.

The founding auction was not even that machine. Overture's, in late 2001, was pure first-price — ads ranked in descending order of bid, each advertiser paying its own bid, averaging about $0.23 a click. The "neutral" second-price ideal was a later, Google-era design, not an original state of grace. Overture's door was also gated: its listings needed human editorial approval. AdWords let the buyer in without one.

Then the seller tuned the price. In 2019, Google changed its search auction — the design the court later called rGSP — and its own pre-launch tests raised the top-slot cost per click by 5.91% on PCs and tablets and 4.85% on mobile. Advertisers could not opt out. In 2024, a federal court, in its findings of fact, named three pricing controls on the search text-ad auction — one called internally "Butternut Squash," plus format pricing and rGSP — as "intentional pricing." The squashing setting, the court noted, has never been publicly disclosed. The same court found that Google had tested whether it could raise text-ad prices by 5% or more without losing many advertisers, and wrote: "the results have been largely consistent — it can."

One fact cuts against reading this as pure extraction. The cut does not only ratchet upward. On Google's syndicated inventory, the take rate rose from about 9% in 2002 to a peak of 24.7% in 2006 — and then fell to 21.3% by 2008, as competition for partners intensified. A pure maximiser would not let its own rate fall.

## The state moves the top of the stack

Concentration in this market is built with capital. The state moves it around and caps it, and only rarely breaks it.

The capital point is plain in the numbers. Overture led the paid-search business on revenue entering the 2000s — $667.7 million in 2002, against Google's $439.5 million in total revenue. The leader had the bigger business, and the corpus does not credit Google with inventing the per-click auction. Overture led it and built it.

The state's record is relocation and ceilings. In 1943 a third national network came into being by sale, not by construction. Edward J. Noble bought NBC's Blue Network for $8 million on 12 October 1943. An incumbent's spare network changed hands; a rival did not build one. (What forced that sale is not in this record, so I state only the sale.) From 1954 the FCC capped any single owner at seven television stations, no more than five of them VHF — a standing ceiling. After 1984 the regional Bell companies became the main publishers of the Yellow Pages. They sold $9,517 million of advertising in 1993, more than radio or magazines: the state minting fresh sellers.

Audience targeting itself entered by purchase, not by build. Google agreed to buy DoubleClick for $3.1 billion in April 2007. Microsoft bought aQuantive for about $6 billion. Yahoo bought Right Media for $680 million. WPP bought 24/7 Real Media for $649 million. Those were the largest such deals of that year, not typical ones.

One deconcentration was real, and the state licensed it without designing it: a postwar boom roughly tripled the AM dial, from about 900 stations in 1945 to roughly 2,500 by the early 1950s.

And when the state named the modern structure unlawful, it did so without pulling it apart. In 2024 a court found that Google had paid $26.3 billion in search default revenue share in 2021. It ruled that spending had foreclosed competition. It also found that Google charged prices above competitive levels for search text ads — "supracompetitive," in the court's word. The finding named the conduct. It did not dismantle the machine.

## Every new unit is born uncounted

Here is the engine underneath all of it, and it is structural, not a scheme. Every new carrier ships a new priced unit that no outside body can yet count. So the seller counts it first. This is my reading of the mechanism; it is adjacency in the record, not a stated law.

The clearest evidence is the historian's own ruler going blind. The long Coen/McCann series tracked US advertising spending from 1919. At the end it understates internet advertising by about half — $10,529 million for 2007, against the industry's own $21,206 million — and then it simply stops, at 2007. The standard measure went dark exactly as the new unit arrived.

The medium-conquest story fails on the same data. Television entered the series in 1949 at $58 million, 1.1% of the total. Total television did not pass newspapers until 1992, and then by about 1% — $31,079 million against $30,737 million. Shares moved slowly. No medium killed the last one in a single stroke. Internet advertising did grow fast once someone counted it: from $23,448 million in 2008 to $294,593 million in 2025, about twelve and a half times, with a single down year in 2009. And "the internet killed classified" is too simple. Newspaper help-wanted classified did collapse — down 91% from its 2000 peak. But real-estate classified peaked in 2006 and fell with the housing market, not with search.

## Which total?

Every "share of advertising" is a claim about a basis. It is not a plain fact, and direct mail is the tell.

Direct mail was the second-largest named medium in most years the data splits by medium — 14.5% of spending in 1949, and a peak of 16.8% in 1938. Yet the 2025 headline total leaves it out. US advertising in 2025 ran about $405 billion on the industry's media-owner-revenue basis — MAGNA's figure is $398 billion — and that basis excludes direct mail entirely. The biggest old channel is written out of the new total.

The rails disagree by more than the fine print admits. On the same media-owner basis, the "national brand" pool for 2025 is about $145.7 billion, 36.6% of MAGNA's $398 billion — the largest or joint-largest pool, on a wide, low-confidence estimate. Change the rail and the whole picture moves: the IRS recorded $473 billion of corporate advertising deductions for 2022, against MAGNA's $325 billion for the same year. That is a $148 billion gap between two counts of the same thing. Any chart of this has to name its rail on the axis, not in a footnote. There is no single US advertising total waiting to be found.

Intensity over time tells the same cautionary story. On the long Coen series, advertising peaked at 3.0% of GDP in 1922 and bottomed at 1.2% in 1944. A 2025 reading puts it near 1.32% of GDP. But that last number sits on a different basis and cannot be lined up against the old series; the fall is partly real and partly a change in what counts as advertising at all.

## Back to the hole

The count is still a hole, and the hole is where we started.

The industry knows it. The IAB estimates that better, AI-driven measurement could unlock more than $26 billion of misallocated US media spending within a year or two — its own reading of how much money the measurement failure is moving to the wrong places. That is an estimate of what might be recovered, not a settled figure.

Return to the opener. Google still reports the growth of its paid clicks, not their number. And the model is reappearing inside the answer box. Pew found that users clicked a search result on 8% of visits where an AI summary appeared, against 15% of visits where none did. Google disputes that study, so both figures stand, unresolved. Google's AI Overviews reached more than two billion monthly users by July 2025, and Google's chief business officer said the format earns money at approximately the same rate as ordinary search — his account, not an audited fact.

Here is the reading the record leaves, stated plainly and owned as a reading. The platform era is not a new medium's conquest. It is the condition of 1913 at a far larger scale — the party being judged owning the number — but without the outside auditor the market built in 1914 and then let go. No source says this in one sentence. It is the shape the layers make when you stack them.

And there is no ending to report. No source records a single moment when the whole market saw this and turned. Reform came in pieces — a government audit here, a rating council there — era by era, never all at once. The count is still a hole. The story is still running.
