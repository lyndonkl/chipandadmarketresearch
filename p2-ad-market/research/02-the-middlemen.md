# The Middlemen (1840s-1917)

In 1840 no advertising industry existed. A gap did.

A soap maker in Ohio could not learn which papers a farmer in Iowa read. He could not learn what space in them cost. If a publisher quoted him a price, he had no way to test it. Closing that gap was worth money.

The people who closed it were not writers or artists. They were brokers. What they built, and who paid them to build it, is the story.

## The middleman worked for the seller

Volney B. Palmer set up in Philadelphia in the early 1840s. Sources put the year at 1840, 1841 or 1842, so the record does not crown him the first agency. It calls him the first, or among the first.[^1]

The year matters less than the money. Publishers paid Palmer a commission most sources put at about 25 percent of the space price he sold.[^1] A commission here just means a cut of the sale.

Hold on to that. The middleman worked for the seller. He earned more when the buyer paid more.

Palmer sold his reach hard. By 1849 his agency claimed to be the sole representative of 1,300 of the roughly 2,000 papers then published in America.[^2] That is the agency advertising itself, so treat it as a sales pitch. The true count may sit well below the claim. Our range runs from 700 to 1,400 papers.

The price of the middleman was not fixed either. Before any standard existed, the commission an agent took ran anywhere between about 10 and 50 percent of the space price.[^3] Publishers did not hold to their printed rates. Agents undercut each other. Nobody could check anybody.

That is the whole era in one sentence: a market with no price and no count.

## First, somebody had to give the news away

The seller side made the deeper invention, and made it early.

On 3 September 1833, Benjamin Day launched the New York Sun at one cent. That was below what it cost him to print. He said so on his own front page, promising cheap news to readers and a good medium to advertisers. Within about two years he was selling roughly 15,000 copies a day.[^4] Our range on that count is wide, from 8,000 to 20,000.

Every ad-funded business since has re-run that bargain. Give the audience the product under cost. Sell the audience.

Magazines ran the same trade again at the end of the century. Frank Munsey cut Munsey's Magazine from 25 cents to 10 cents in 1893, again below the cost of making it. His advertising then brought in roughly $25,000 to $35,000 an issue.[^5] Munsey himself said the ten-cent magazine grew the magazine-buying public from about 250,000 people to about 750,000 between 1893 and 1899.[^6]

Note who is talking. That is the man who invented the ten-cent magazine, describing his own effect, and no independent count exists. We grade it low and widen it out: the 1899 figure could be anywhere from 450,000 to 950,000.

The shift shows up in publisher accounts. Advertising supplied about 44 percent of US newspaper revenue in 1879.[^7] By 1914 it supplied 64.9 percent, about $184 million of advertising against $99.5 million from copies sold.[^8] Across those years the reader went from being most of the business to less than half of it.

## The open contract turns the agent around

In 1875, N. W. Ayer & Son offered advertisers a different deal, called the open contract.[^9] The advertiser handed over a budget instead of a media order. The agent bought whatever space he judged best. The rate was disclosed, and the agent's cut was a fixed, agreed percentage.

That single change flipped the middleman to the buyer's side of the table.

It also broke the agency's old product. Once an agent could no longer make money by hiding the price, he had to sell something else. Agencies invented the rest of the job instead: market research first, then copy and art. By the 1900s the writing was the product.

The cut itself drifted, then settled. It stabilised at 15 percent of the gross rate by the early 1890s, and the open contract carrying that 15 percent became standard practice through that decade.[^10] Gross rate means the full list price of the space, before the agent's cut comes out.

Then publishers enforced it. From 1893, publishers agreed to pay commission only to agents they recognised, and never to advertisers who bought direct.[^11] A customary rate became a defended one. An advertiser could not save the 15 percent by cutting out the agent, because the discount was simply not available to him.

The middleman's cut at the end of this era is clean: the agency took 15 percent of gross billings, and the publisher kept the other 85 percent.[^11] Billings are the full amount the advertiser was charged. That cut is what this series calls a take rate. Two honest caveats travel with the number. The agency trade body only wrote 15 percent into an industry-wide standard in 1918, just outside this era. And the agency was the main cut in the chain, but not provably the only one, since publishers also paid sales representatives at rates this record does not establish.

Publishers quoted their prices by the line. Ladies' Home Journal raised its rate to $2.00 per agate line in 1887, which worked out to $28 a column inch, and was earning about $500,000 a year from advertising by 1897.[^12] An agate line is one line of very small type, one column wide. Space was sold the way cloth is sold, by the running unit.

## Who was actually paying

Four separate pools of money bought advertising in this era. They are still four pools today.

**Patent medicine came first.** Cure-all tonics had no shops and no salesmen, so print was the entire business. Patent medicine was Ayer's most lucrative category in 1878, at 26 percent of the agency's revenue.[^13] By 1900 it had slipped to second place at 15 percent, as branded packaged goods took the lead.[^14]

**Mail order bought advertising the way nobody else could: by the order.** Montgomery Ward's first catalogue, in 1872, was one page listing 163 items.[^15] By 1897 it ran to nearly 1,000 pages and supported about $7 million of sales a year, and by 1904 Ward was mailing roughly 3 million catalogues a year.[^16] Sears passed Ward in 1900, at about $10 million against Ward's $8.7 million.[^17] By 1908 Sears was selling about $40.8 million a year, after a dip in the Panic of 1907.[^18]

Two postal changes did the heavy lifting. Third-class mail, the cheap class that carried circulars and catalogues, grew from an estimated 301 million pieces in 1880 to more than six billion by 1930.[^19] Parcel Post arrived on 1 January 1913, and Sears filled five times as many orders that year as the year before.[^20]

**Local retail was the biggest buyer of newspaper space, by a lot.** In 1915, national advertisers bought $55 million of newspaper space. Local advertisers, counting classified, bought $220 million.[^21] Local money was four times national money, and national money was only about a fifth of newspaper advertising.[^22] The national brand was the new buyer in this era, not the big one.

**Classified was the fourth pool, and the odd one.** Newspapers sold it by the line over a counter, to individuals and very small firms, with no agent involved. It was probably around 13 percent of newspaper advertising receipts in 1914, but that one is genuinely uncertain. No dollar series for classified exists before 1928, so the estimate is built from later line counts and one in-era witness. The honest range is 8 to 22 percent.[^23]

One pool crossed all the others. Response-driven money was already large. In 1911 the trade paper *Printers' Ink* counted $100 million of direct mail, $75 million of farm and mail-order advertising, and about $31 million more on free samples, handouts and company magazines. Those lines sum to $206 million, and that is not our figure. We take only 65% of the mail line as response money, carry the rest to 1914, and blend the result with three other routes. Our figure is about $192 million, set as 16.0% of the $1,200 million benchmark for 1914.[^24]

## How big was all of it

Here the record has to slow down, because the numbers get soft.

No annual series exists for this era, and nobody can honestly build one. Nothing at all exists before 1867. After that the record holds only a set of benchmark years, each one estimated long after the fact.

Worse, two credible sets of estimates disagree with each other. Robert Coen compiled the older set for Printers' Ink, and the Census Bureau carried it into its historical statistics. Coen later revised his own numbers down by roughly 13 to 19 percent for the same years. We carry both lineages and widen the range, rather than quietly splitting the difference.

The 80 percent range is the band we would give roughly an eight-in-ten chance of holding the true value. For 1914 the two lineages read $1,100 million and $1,302 million.[^25] For 1917 they read $1,380 million and $1,627 million.[^26] The gap between them is not a rounding error. It is the size of a whole medium.

One number in this era is solid. The Census of Manufactures counted what publishers actually received: $255.6 million of advertising receipts in 1914 across all newspapers and periodicals.[^27] Newspapers took about $184 million of that, and everything else in print shared the rest, so newspapers held 72 percent of print.[^28]

Now compare the two. The benchmark totals run several times higher than census receipts for the same year. That is not a contradiction, and it is the single most important thing to understand about this era's numbers.

The census counts money publishers received. The benchmark counts money advertisers spent. The gap between them is agency commission, the cost of making the ads, and every kind of promotion that never touches a publisher. Coen's own newspaper line runs about 1.52 times census newspaper receipts, and that factor is documented rather than guessed.[^29] Even after scaling, all of print reaches only about 21 percent of the broad benchmark total for 1914, and possibly as little as 17 percent.[^30]

The pre-1919 totals are therefore not a media measure. They are a promotional-spending measure, on a wider basis than any modern series. Any chart that runs this era into the next has to say so at the seam.

### Where the money sat, by type

Splitting that total by money type is a modelling job, not a measurement. All four figures below are our weakest grade, and the ranges are wide on purpose.

| Money type, 1914 | Best estimate | 80% range | Claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| National brand | 22% | 11-46% | e1-scale-011 |
| Local retail, excluding classified | 22% | 15-46% | e1-scale-012 |
| Direct response | 16% | 8-30% | e1-scale-014 |
| Classified | 2.2% | 1.1-5% | e1-scale-013 |
| Not assigned to any of the four | 37.8% | 5-55% | e1-scale-017 |

Read the shape, not the digits. National brand and local retail tie at the top, and no estimating method we tried can tell them apart.[^31] Every method that lifts one lowers the other, because the two share the same newspaper total. An earlier draft called direct response the largest money type in the era. It is not, and neither is any other pool: the only ranking this evidence supports is that classified was the smallest.

Classified was small in dollars, at roughly 2.2 percent of the total, or about $26 million.[^32] Its importance is not its size. It is the only channel here bought self-serve, over a counter, by people who never met an agent and never paid the 15 percent. It stayed small partly because of how it was priced. Classified paid no agency cut and needed no plates or art, so a dollar of classified space cost a dollar, while a dollar of brand space came with about half a dollar of preparation behind it.

The four shares reach only 62 percent of the whole, and we do not force them higher. About 5 to 9 points of the rest is a real fifth pool with a real name: trade and technical papers, legal and financial notices, institutional and political money. The remaining 29 to 33 points is not a money type at all. It is the gap between two counts of the same year, and the second count needs introducing here.

*Printers' Ink* tabulated twelve advertising channels in 1911, and its twelve lines add to $616 million. We grew that forward to 1914 using the census growth in newspaper receipts, then added window display and directory listings, which brings it to about $700 million. A 1914 textbook by Daniel Starch put yearly spending on printed advertising at nearly the same figure.[^51] That is the only count of advertising by channel made inside the era.

The $700 million and the census $255.6 million do not compete. The census counts what newspapers and periodicals received. The channel count is wider: it carries print space as well as direct mail, mail order, outdoor, house organs, samples and novelties. Neither one is the benchmark. The retrospective benchmark, worked out decades later, is $1,200 million, and nobody has ever explained the $500 million between it and the channel count. We show that gap rather than spread it.

The types also overlap. Patent-medicine and mail-order money was national and response-driven at once, and this record counts it as direct response, because that is how the buyer judged it.

### The share-of-GDP trap

Advertising took roughly 3.3 percent of everything the US economy produced in 1914, up from about 1.9 percent in 1880 and about 0.6 percent in 1867.[^33] Economists call that total GDP, and the later chapters use the word. Our range on the 1914 figure runs from 2.6 to 4.0 percent.

That looks like a clean story of advertising eating a growing share of the economy. Resist it. Somebody had to rebuild both numbers in that fraction. The top is the broad outlay concept described above. The bottom is a modern rebuild of pre-1929 GDP, because official national accounts do not start until 1929.

That matters straight away. The next era records about 3.0 percent in 1922 as the peak of the whole later series.[^34] Both statements are true inside their own windows. They are not points on one line, and nobody should draw one through them.

## Nobody was counting the audience

The thing being bought was circulation, meaning the number of copies a publication sold. For most of this era, publishers simply stated their own circulation and advertisers had no way to check.

Every publisher printed a price list for its space, called a rate card. That is why the rate card was only an asking price. You cannot enforce a price for something nobody has counted.

The market was also enormously fragmented, which made checking harder. The United States had 22,754 newspapers and periodicals in 1914.[^35] Their combined circulation ran to 205.6 million copies per issue, up 25 percent on the 1909 count.[^36] No seller had power over a national audience. That fragmentation is exactly why brokers could exist at all.

Counting arrived in three waves.

**Commercial first.** George P. Rowell published his American Newspaper Directory in 1869, listing 5,778 American papers with his own estimated circulations.[^37] It was the first attempt at a shared currency for buying space. A currency here means an agreed number both sides will trade on.

**Then government.** The Newspaper Publicity Act of August 1912 tied the cheap postal rate for publications to a sworn statement of circulation for daily papers, and required paid reading matter to be labelled as advertising.[^38] The Supreme Court upheld it the next year.

**Then the industry itself.** In 1914, in Chicago, a publisher-led audit body and an advertiser-led one merged into the Audit Bureau of Circulations, the world's first third-party media audit.[^39] It promised facts without opinions and could suspend a member that padded its numbers. Members filed statements twice a year and were audited once a year. Within a dozen years it covered 925 newspapers, 184 general magazines, 240 business publications and 72 farm papers.[^40]

Look at who paid for it. Advertisers, agencies and publishers funded the audit together. Every measurement currency since has copied that three-sided design, ratings and impressions included.

That audit is the era's real climax. From 1914, a rate card rests on a number somebody else checked. That is the moment the price becomes a contract term instead of an opening bid.

Running alongside it was a second, private way of counting. Mail-order firms keyed their coupons, printing a code on each one so a reply could be traced back to the paper and the headline that produced it. That counts replies rather than readers. It is the direct ancestor of the click, and it never went away.

## Targeting meant picking a magazine

Nobody in this era targeted a person. They targeted a title and a place.

A farm weekly reached farmers. A religious paper reached churchgoers. A city daily reached one city. The buyer's menu of choices grew about fourfold, from 5,778 listed papers in 1869 to 22,754 counted publications in 1914.[^41] Directories became a channel of their own once phone companies sorted their listings by trade.

Two sharper forms existed. The catalogue was addressed to a named household, which made it the only individually addressable medium of the era, and Ward alone was mailing about 3 million a year by 1904.[^42] And classified let readers sort themselves. Someone who wanted a job or a room went to the column where those offers sat. That is intent matching, done by hand, decades before a search box.

Outside print, sellers were building their own reach. Barron Collier's firm sold card space inside about 21,000 streetcars across 35 states by 1905, the largest streetcar advertising business in the country.[^43]

## The trust crisis that built the rules

The era's biggest advertiser category was also its biggest liability.

Ladies' Home Journal stopped taking patent-medicine advertising in 1892, giving up real revenue on trust grounds. In October 1905, Samuel Hopkins Adams began a series in Collier's showing that the leading cure-alls were alcohol, opiates and poisons, and naming the papers that carried them. The Pure Food and Drug Act followed in 1906.[^44]

The trade then wrote rules on itself. Printers' Ink commissioned a model law against false advertising in 1911. By November 1921, 22 states had adopted that model law almost unchanged, and 15 more had adopted a modified version.[^45] The Federal Trade Commission Act was signed in 1914, and the first deceptive-advertising case followed in 1916.[^50]

Advertising did not become respectable by growing up. It became respectable because Congress forced its biggest customer to change.

## The era closes on a fixed price

The agency trade body arrived last. The American Association of Advertising Agencies was founded on 4 June 1917, at City Hall in St. Louis, with 111 charter member agencies drawn from five regional groups.[^46]

The layer it represented was still small and scattered. N. W. Ayer, one of the largest agencies in the country, billed $18.58 million in 1917, up from $10.41 million in 1913.[^47] Set against the national total, that is roughly 1.2 percent of everything spent on advertising in America, and plausibly anywhere from 0.9 to 1.7 percent.[^48] Even a leading agency handled only a sliver of the whole.

The era ends with the middleman's price fixed, the audience audited, and the money running through small firms that publishers approved and that policed themselves.

## Three things the record refused to say

Being honest about what we do not know is part of the story, so here is where the evidence killed a good line.

**The Wanamaker quote.** Every history of advertising has the department-store owner saying that half his advertising was wasted and he did not know which half. No primary source for it exists, and the earliest credit to him is a 1919 sermon, preached two years after this era closes. Chapter 3 follows the chain.[^49]

**The first advertising agency.** Palmer is dated 1840, 1841 or 1842 depending on the source, and later firms have competing claims. The record says first or among the first, and stops there.[^1]

**Barron Collier's market share.** An early draft said the streetcar-card firm controlled over three quarters of that market. That figure could not be sourced anywhere, so it was cut. The streetcar count survived the check; the share did not.[^43]

## What this era hands to the next

Four things cross into 1918 intact.

A price: 15 percent of gross billings, defended by publisher recognition, and about to be written into an industry standard.

A currency: audited circulation, funded by all three sides, print-only. Radio will arrive in the next era with no currency at all, which is why its first pricing mechanism had to be something else entirely.

Four money types, all already present: national brand, local retail, classified and direct response. None of them is a later invention. Classified, directories and direct mail are all pre-1918 businesses, and they need to be tracked from here forward.

And one buyer class worth marking. The classified counter and the mail-order coupon were self-serve, agency-free and judged on measured replies. Small buyers used them without ever paying a commission. That buyer never had a national medium to spend on. Much later, a search auction handed him one.
