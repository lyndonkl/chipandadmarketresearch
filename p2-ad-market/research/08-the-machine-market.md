# The Machine Market (2008-2026)

Era 7. Evidence freeze: 30 June 2026.

## The short version

In 2008 a person still bought most ads. By 2026 a machine bought them, and often wrote them too.

Three jobs moved into software in this era, in order. Buying went first. Then pricing. Then the ad itself. And the firm that ran the auction was usually the same firm that counted the result. That overlap is the era's central fact. Most of its failures follow from it.

The era also killed the auction, the mechanism the last one was named for. Not by replacing it. By tuning it.

## The plumbing

Google closed its purchase of DoubleClick on 11 March 2008, for $3.1B [e7-events-001]. The price was not the point. The assets were. Google now owned the software that publishers used to decide which ad to show, plus the machinery for trading picture ads on ordinary web pages. That machinery became Google's own exchange: a marketplace where ad slots are bought and sold.

A year later that exchange relaunched with real-time bidding. Each single ad slot now went to auction as the page loaded. Buyers could bring their own data and their own models to each one. The industry wrote an open protocol for it soon after, so the method belonged to the market rather than to one firm.

It arrived in a bad year. US internet ad revenue fell 3% in 2009. That is the only fall in the whole era [e7-events-002].

"Programmatic" is just a name for this: buying ad slots by software and by rule, one at a time, instead of by phone call and a signed paper order. It won almost completely. By 2025, about 90% of all US non-search digital ad revenue was traded this way [e7-scale-005].

## The money and the medium

US internet ad revenue was $23,448M in 2008 [e7-scale-004]. In 2025 it was $294,593M. That is 12.6 times larger in seventeen years [e7-medium-001].

The 2025 split, by format: search $114.2B, display $81.6B, digital video $78.0B, digital audio $8.4B, and $12.5B in an "other" bucket that holds classifieds, directories and lead generation [e7-medium-004].

How much of all US ad money is now digital? We estimate 74%, but the honest range is wide: somewhere between 72% and 82%, depending on whose universe you count [e7-medium-002]. The total itself is contested the same way. About $405B in 2025, though the two firms that estimate it disagree, MAGNA reading $398B and EMARKETER $422B for what is supposed to be the same country and year [e7-scale-001]. MAGNA forecasts the market from inside Interpublic, one of the big agency groups, which buys media for advertisers. EMARKETER is a private research firm that sells its estimates by subscription to anyone in the trade. Globally the gap is wider still: roughly $1.05T, with MAGNA at $979B and WPP Media at $1.14T [e7-scale-003]. WPP Media buys media for advertisers too, inside WPP, the group that took over J. Walter Thompson in 1987. Both global readings come from the buyers' side of the market.

Set that against the size of the economy and something odd appears. Measured US ad spend was about 1.32% of GDP in 2025 [e7-scale-002]. In 2007 the older Coen series read 2.0%. The measured ad market shrank as a share of the economy while the platforms grew. That is a real puzzle, and this chapter does not settle it. Two warnings belong with the number. It is a grade C estimate. And the two years come from different series that do not count the same things, so part of the fall is a change in what "advertising" means.

Do not read that 1.32% as the fall from a dot-com peak. Chapter 3 already put the high point in 1922, and 2000 ranks well down the list.

Older channels kept shrinking on schedule. Directory advertising, the Yellow Pages business, fell from a peak near $14.7B in 2005 to roughly $1.1B in 2024, though no audited series survives to pin it down; the honest range is $0.7B to $2.0B [e7-medium-006]. Direct mail was still worth about $37.3B in 2024, but USPS Marketing Mail volume fell from 99 billion pieces in 2008 to 56.8 billion in 2025 [e7-buyers-004]. The same money now buys far fewer, far better aimed pieces.

Two channels moved the other way. Connected TV reached about $33.35B in 2025 [e7-medium-005]. It is where brand television money finally crossed into an auction. Podcast revenue grew from $105.7M in 2015 to $2,862.2M in 2025 [e7-medium-007].

Whose money is it? No modern source publishes the split by money type, so this record rebuilds it. All four figures are grade C and the bands are wide. Every one of them is measured against MAGNA's $398B, which counts what media owners took in and leaves direct mail out, so direct mail is out of all four pools too. National direct response: about $151B, or 38%, and somewhere between $117B and $187B [e7-scale-009]. National brand money: about $146B, or 37%, and somewhere between $100B and $187B [e7-scale-006]. Local and retail buyers: about $94B, or 24%, and somewhere between $60B and $123B [e7-scale-007]. Classified and directory money: about $10B, or 2.5% [e7-scale-008]. The four run 0.6 points over the total, and we report that rather than shave it [e7-scale-010].

The top two cannot be ranked. Their centres sit 1.4 points apart and their ranges nearly coincide, and the three methods behind them do not even agree on which is bigger. The record does say that response money and brand money are now the two big pools, that local money is a clear third, and that classified is a distant fourth.

An earlier version of this chapter said brand money had become the smallest of the four. That was wrong. We made a bookkeeping error, not a judgement call. The local figure it subtracted was $169B from BIA Advisory Services, a research firm that measures local advertising and sells the detail by subscription. That total counts everyone who buys local audiences, national chains included. BIA's own split puts $116B of that with national and regional advertisers and only $59B with small businesses.

## Death one: header bidding

The auction died three times in this era. The first death was not Google's doing.

Publishers used to sell leftover ad space through a waterfall. The ad server called one exchange, offered the slot at a price, and moved to the next exchange only if the first declined. The order was fixed and the publisher set it. That order was worth a great deal to whoever sat at the top of it, because a buyer further down never saw what the buyer above had passed on.

Header bidding broke that. The publisher put a small piece of code in the page header. Every exchange bid at the same time, before the ad server was called. The trade press dates the break to 2014 and 2015, and an open-source project called Prebid.js turned the trick into a standard.

Two things ended at once. The waterfall's queue of falling prices was over. And Google's ad server, which had enjoyed the first call, now had to win on price like everyone else.

## Deaths two and three: the 2019 fork

Then came 2019, and this is where most accounts go wrong.

Two changes landed that year. They ran in opposite directions, on opposite sides of the same company.

**Display went to first price.** On 5 September 2019 Google finished moving Ad Manager, its open-web display exchange, to a unified first-price auction [mech-first_price-001]. In a second-price auction the winner pays just enough to beat the runner-up. In a first-price auction the winner pays what it bid. Google's stated reason was a fair and transparent market under header-bidding pressure. It also folded publisher price floors, the least a publisher would accept for a slot, into one set of rules at the same time. On revenue it reported only that testing showed a "neutral to positive" effect for publishers.

That modest claim is the correct one, and the mechanism explains why. A first-price rule is not a money machine. Buyers respond by shading: they bid below their true value, by an amount that depends on how many rivals they expect. Work through the simple case where every buyer is alike, and the two rules pay the seller exactly the same, whatever the number of bidders. The first-price rule holds no yield mechanism at all.

The popular version of this story quotes a frozen set of bids under both rules and reports a large windfall for first price. That reading is an artefact. It assumes bidders keep bidding the same numbers under a rule that punishes them for it. They do not.

The real change in 2019 was who works out the winning price. Under second price the seller computed it from the runner-up's bid. Under first price the buyer's own model computes it. That is the transparency argument, and it has force, because the party computing the old second price also ran the exchange and the publisher's ad server.

Three cautions go with that result. The equal-revenue finding holds only in a textbook case, and the real display market broke it in three places. Buyers were not alike; they had different data and different rules for how far to bid below their value. Google computed the old second price, and Google ran the exchange. And Google moved the floors at the same time, so nobody can separate the two effects in publisher revenue. The defensible claim is the negative one, and it is strong: nothing in the first-price rule mechanically raises yield.

**Search went the other way.** In the same year, Google launched rGSP on search. The letters stand for randomized generalized second-price. It shuffles the order of ads with similar scores, so a lower-ranked ad can sometimes appear above a higher-ranked one. That breaks the reliability of a trick sophisticated bidders had used: bidding down a slot to pay less. Break the trick, and bidders stop shading as hard.

The record on this is unusually good, because it came out in court. Pre-launch tests raised the top-slot cost per click by 5.91% on PCs and tablets, and 4.85% on mobile. Google expected 40% to 50% of the gain to stick. Two months after launch a 5.74% revenue gain was still there. Advertisers cannot opt out [e7-pricing-001, mech-rgsp-001]. The stated reason in the court's findings was revenue. Not transparency. Not fairness. Revenue.

**Search never went first-price.** The opposite claim is the most common factual error in retellings of 2019. Any sentence that says "the ad market moved to first price in 2019" is a sentence about display. On search, the 2019 move went toward more seller control, not less.

## The knobs

The court record named three levers Google used on the search auction. Google called them "intentional pricing" in-house [mech-knobs-001].

**Squashing**, launched under the name "Butternut Squash", compresses the quality term in the ranking formula. The old search auction ranked ads by bid times predicted click-through rate. Squashing shrinks the gap between a good click rate and a bad one. The winner can stay the same while its price rises toward the runner-up's raw bid. Push the dial hard enough and the ranking flips back to the crude pure-bid order, which is worse for the searcher and better for the seller on the same impression. Google has never disclosed the exponent, so no outside party can reproduce its effect.

**Format pricing** charges more for the shape of an ad rather than for its position. It had grown to about 20% of Google's search text-ads revenue per thousand queries before rGSP replaced it. Individual experiments launched at about +15% and kept roughly half of that for the long term [e7-pricing-002, mech-format_pricing-001]. A handful of launches that size compound to a share that large. None of it touches ranking, ad copy or relevance.

**rGSP** is the third, described above.

On top of the three sat routine tuning. Google's VP of ads testified that the firm moved auction and reserve prices to hit quarterly revenue targets. The moves ran about 5% on average, and up to 10% on some queries. Google did not tell advertisers [e7-pricing-003, mech-tuning-001].

Two points make sense of this, and both cut against the tidy story of a good auction gone bad.

First, it is not new. From August 2005 Google set minimum bids per keyword using its own quality score [mech-quality_score-001]. On a query with one bidder, the minimum bid is the price. Moving it is a direct price decision by the seller, with no rival involved. The seller has been posting prices on the long tail since the middle of the auction years.

Second, the knobs did not have to be large. The old second-price auction already left the seller a wide band, because what an advertiser pays depends on how well it plays, and most play badly. The seller sits on that band. A 5% or 6% nudge moves the market a short way inside it. The mechanism did not have to be abandoned to become a price-setting instrument. Google only had to tune it, and Google never told anyone.

That leaves the governance point, which is the same in 2005 and in 2019. The seller computed every number that decides who wins and what they pay: the predicted click-through rate, the quality reserve, the squashing exponent, the format multiplier, the test for whether a click was real. It published none of them. Nobody audited them. The first industry standard for counting a click did not arrive until 2009 [mech-audit-001]. The design case for the auction is sound. The governance case never existed.

## What the middle keeps

If software buys the ad, software also takes a cut at every hop. Three studies, three angles, one answer.

A UK supply-chain study traced advertiser money through to publishers. Only 51% arrived. The researchers could not trace a further 15% to any named party in the chain, and could follow only 12% of impressions end to end [e7-pricing-004].

A second study traced money through the demand-side platform, the software a buyer uses to bid for ad slots. Only about 36 cents of each dollar put in bought an ad a real person saw. Fees took 29 cents and waste took the other 35. The study sized the pool at $88B and named about $22B of that, a quarter, as gains a buyer could win back. Do not read the $22B as the other side of the 36 cents. That side is 64 cents on the dollar, or about $56B. Made-for-advertising sites, which exist to carry ads rather than readers, took about 15% of spend on the first look and 11.4% on the full report. The 21 firms studied are American, but nobody could confirm whether the $88B pool is American or worldwide [e7-pricing-005].

Google published its own figure in June 2020: publishers keep about 69 cents of an advertiser dollar when both ends of the trade sit inside Google. The US Justice Department's number for the same market is different. It says Google keeps at least 35 cents of every open-web display dollar, and that its exchange has charged a 20% take rate since 2009 [e7-pricing-006].

## The signals get cut

Targeting in this era ran a full arc: from the finest-grained tracking ever built, through two deliberate cuts, to something coarser and far more powerful.

The European Union's privacy law, the GDPR, took effect in May 2018. It required people to agree before firms could collect the browsing records that ad targeting ran on. The measured effects are real, modest and contested. One large-publisher study found click-through down 2.1% and revenue per click down 5.7%. Another found roughly 10% falls in recorded page views and revenue for EU users. A publisher survey found no aggregate fall at all [e7-targeting-003]. Keep the disagreement; it is the finding.

Apple's App Tracking Transparency shipped with iOS 14.5 in April 2021. Apps now had to ask before tracking users across other firms' apps and sites. Opt-in ran about 16% at launch and rose to roughly 25% by mid-2022 [e7-targeting-002]. Meta, the company behind Facebook and Instagram and the second-largest ad seller in America, told investors the change would cost it "on the order of $10 billion" in 2022, and outside estimates ran as high as $12.8B [e7-targeting-001]. Note what this was. Not a law. A rule written by one firm, Apple, that cost Meta more than any privacy law had.

The third shock never landed. Google announced in January 2020 that it would retire the third-party cookie within two years. It delayed three times. In July 2024 it said it would not deprecate the cookie unilaterally, and in April 2025 it dropped even the choice prompt. The cookie outlived the decade of preparation for its funeral. That non-event is the honest finding, and it matters for what comes next.

## Nobody counts the counter

For a century the market's discipline was simple. A third party counted the audience, and the buyer could audit someone other than the seller. That rule broke here.

Facebook overstated average video view time for about two years by excluding views under three seconds. Plaintiffs said the method inflated the number by up to 900%. It paid $40M in October 2019 to settle the advertiser class action [e7-measurement-001].

The counting institutions took damage from the other side too. The Media Rating Council suspended Nielsen's national and local TV accreditation in September 2021, after finding pandemic-era undercounts of 2% to 6% among adults under 50. A broadcaster trade body put the cost to networks at $468M or more. National accreditation came back in April 2023, nineteen months later [e7-measurement-002].

Programmatic added a counting crisis of its own, and the count of it comes from the sellers themselves. The IAB, their trade body, has measured the internet's ad money since 1996. By 2026 it reported that bots make up over half of global web traffic, with nearly 40% of it malicious [e7-measurement-003]. The same body estimates that better measurement could recover more than $26B of US media money now going to the wrong places [e7-measurement-005]. That is a direct price tag on the failure.

Then privacy law removed the tracking records that let a buyer tie a sale back to the ad that caused it, and buyers retreated to older, coarser tools. Media mix models infer what worked from spending and sales history rather than from tracking one person. Holdout tests keep the ads from part of the audience and compare. The market changed how it knows what worked, not just which software it ran.

Who pays the counter now? Increasingly the seller, or nobody.

## Two sellers, then three

The seller side concentrated, then re-concentrated around a new pole.

The top ten sellers took 84.1% of US internet ad revenue in 2025, up from 78.6% in 2021 [e7-sellers-001]. In absolute terms, Google's worldwide ad revenue was $294,691M in 2025 [e7-sellers-002]. Meta's was $196.18B and Amazon's ad services line was $68.63B [e7-sellers-003].

Stop on that Google figure, because it collides with an earlier one. It sits $98M from the US internet ad revenue given at the top of this chapter, $294,593M. The near-match is a coincidence and nothing more. Google's figure counts every ad dollar the company earned anywhere on earth, YouTube and partner sites included. The US figure counts every internet ad dollar earned inside one country by every seller in it. Neither number is a misprint, and neither one contains the other.

Now a result that looks backwards until you read it twice. Google, Meta and Amazon together took about 71.9% of US digital ad spend in 2025, down from 73.9% in 2020. Over the same years their share of all US ad spend rose from about 47% to about 59% [e7-sellers-004]. Concentration inside digital eased slightly. Concentration of the whole market rose sharply. Digital was eating everything else faster than anyone was eating Google.

Both of those shares come from EMARKETER, and they only hold together on EMARKETER's rail, where digital is about 82% of all US ad money. This chapter counts digital at 74% elsewhere, on the MAGNA and IAB rail. On that rail the same 71.9% of digital works out at 53.2% of the whole market, not 59%. The two readings are $23B apart on one year, so never plot them on one axis or quote them in one sentence.

Amazon is the structural novelty. US retail media reached $60.32B in 2025, and Amazon took more than three quarters of it [e7-sellers-007]. Retail media turns a shop into a media owner selling its own shelf. But the money is mostly not new. It is trade-promotion and co-op money, the manufacturer money that has flowed through retailers for a century. The pricing changed instead. A negotiated allowance became an auction.

Prices were rising as the era closed. Google's cost per click rose 7% in 2025 while paid clicks rose 6% [e7-pricing-007]. Meta's average price per ad rose 9% while impressions rose 12% [e7-pricing-008]. Supply and price rose together on both platforms. In a market with easy entry, that combination is unusual.

## The courts arrive

On 5 August 2024 Judge Mehta held that Google had illegally monopolised general search and general search text advertising. The court found Google held 89.2% of US general search queries in 2020, and 88% of the search text ad market [e7-sellers-005].

The instrument was distribution, not the auction. Google paid $26.3B in search default revenue share in 2021 to be the box people type into [e7-events-009]. It paid Apple $20B in 2022 under one agreement [mech-default-002], on terms reported at about 36% of the search ad revenue Safari generated [mech-default-003]. The challenged contracts covered roughly half of all US general search queries [mech-mehta-001]. Google's own 2017 estimate was that defaults drove 54% of its search revenue [mech-mehta-002]. And it had tested a 5% text-ad price rise and kept it, without losing advertisers of any consequence [mech-mehta-004]. That last fact is what monopoly power looks like as a number.

The remedy came on 2 September 2025. Default payments survived. Exclusivity did not, and the court ordered data sharing with qualified rivals. The court did not order Google to sell Chrome, the web browser it owns and one of the main places it pays to be the default [mech-mehta-005]. The judge wrote that generative AI had changed the course of the case.

A second court reached a second verdict. On 17 April 2025 Judge Brinkema held that Google had monopolised the open-web publisher ad server and ad exchange markets, and had unlawfully tied them together. No remedies order had issued by this record's freeze date. It is an open item, not a result.

Meanwhile the distribution engine kept growing and changed shape. Google paid $59,926M in traffic acquisition cost in 2025, a rate of 20.3% of ad revenue [e7-sellers-006]. In the auction years most of that money bought ad slots on other people's sites. By 2021 most of it, we estimate around 58%, went on search defaults instead [mech-default-004]. That figure is grade C. The direction is not in doubt. Google stopped mainly paying for places to put ads, and started mainly paying to be found.

## The machines start answering

Google launched AI Overviews in May 2024, putting a generated answer above the results. By July 2025 the format had more than 2 billion monthly users, and Google said it earns at about the same rate as classic search [e7-targeting-004].

The cost shows up on the other side of the page. Pew's browsing panel found users clicked a search result on 8% of visits where an AI summary appeared, against 15% of visits without one. They clicked a source cited inside the summary on 1% of visits [e7-measurement-004]. Google disputes the method, and this is a single panel study, so treat it as a signal rather than a settled rate. If it holds, the summary keeps the reader and the open web loses the click.

On the buy side, the advertiser stopped choosing. Google's Performance Max and Meta's Advantage+ take a budget, a goal and a feed of assets, and pick the audience, the placement and the creative themselves. Meta disclosed that its automated ad products had passed a $60B annualised revenue rate [e7-targeting-005]. Adoption of Google's version is harder to pin down: a vendor survey put it at 71% of advertisers, but nobody publishes how many advertisers there are, so our honest range is somewhere between 50% and 85% [e7-buyers-003].

The machine also writes the ad now. Within a month of launching its generative creative tools, Meta said more than a million advertisers had used them to make over 15 million ads [e7-creators-004].

The era closes with targeting moving inside the conversation. Meta began using Meta AI chat interactions for ad targeting in December 2025, with no opt-out. OpenAI's ChatGPT ad pilot went live in February 2026 for US adults on its free and cheapest tiers. Perplexity went the other way, abandoning the sponsored-question product it had launched a year earlier. Two of the three bets are still open at the freeze.

## Who makes the ads now

The people who make ads split three ways in this era.

The holding companies kept merging and kept losing the media-buying job. Omnicom closed its purchase of Interpublic in November 2025, forming a group with combined revenue above $25B [e7-creators-001]. Meanwhile the work left. The Association of National Advertisers, or ANA, is the trade body of the buyers, the firms that pay for the ads. By 2023, 82% of its member marketers had an in-house agency of their own, up from 78% in 2018 [e7-creators-002]. The 15% commission that the first agencies settled on in the 1890s finished dying here [e1-pricing-002]. Its successor is principal media: the agency buys inventory in bulk and resells it to the client at a markup it does not disclose.

The platforms became creators, as the last section showed. And individual creators became a channel of their own. US creator advertising was worth about $37B in 2025, with about $44B projected for 2026, growing at roughly twice the rate of the market [e7-creators-003].

The buyer base widened past anything the old market could serve. Meta has cited more than 10 million active advertisers [e7-buyers-001]. Political money became a cyclical shock of its own, at $11.1B across the 2023-24 US cycle [e7-buyers-002].

## What a query is worth

Era 7 is the first era where we can price the unit itself, and the unit is a search query.

We estimate that Google earned about 3.7 cents of ad revenue per query in 2025, with an honest range of roughly 3 to 5 cents [e7-unit_econ-001]. It is grade C, because the revenue line includes some non-search products and the query count comes from a company statement, not an audit.

What did the query cost? The court record gives us 2020: about 0.65 cents to run the search engine and the search ads business, per query, with a range of 0.4 to 1 cent [e7-unit_econ-002]. Now put the distribution number beside it. The default payments the court examined worked out to roughly 0.8 cents per query. Google paid more per query to be found than it spent answering it. That single comparison explains the antitrust case better than any market-share figure.

The margin, at the segment level, is not in dispute. Google Services earned a 40.7% operating margin in 2025, after paying $59.9B of traffic acquisition cost [e7-unit_econ-003].

Now the comparison this project was built to make. What does it cost a machine to write one answer?

Fix the answer at a standard size, roughly a page of input and a paragraph of output. Then price it twice a year: once at the cheapest capable model on sale, and once at the top model. Those are two lines, not one line with a wide band. Treating them as one band is what hid the next result.

Take the cheap line first. In 2023 the cheapest model of GPT-4 quality cost about 4.8 cents an answer [e7-unit_econ-004]. That is roughly the entire ad revenue of a search query. By July 2024 the same answer cost 0.033 cents at GPT-4o mini [e7-unit_econ-005]. By 2025 it cost about 0.0165 cents at Gemini 2.0 Flash-Lite [e7-unit_econ-006]. At the freeze in mid-2026 it cost about 0.022 cents [e7-unit_econ-007].

Read those last two again. The floor went up. Google shut Gemini 2.0 Flash-Lite down on 1 June 2026, and every cheap model launched since costs more than the floor it replaced. The cheap tier did not merely stop falling. The cheapest price a buyer can reach rose by a third.

The 2023 to 2024 fall is exact rather than estimated. It is one printed list price divided by another: 0.048 over 0.00033, or 145.45 times in twelve months. Both ends name a model, so no band belongs around it.

The top line runs the other way. GPT-4o cost 0.95 cents an answer in 2024, GPT-5 cost 0.43 cents in 2025, and at the freeze GPT-5.4 Standard cost 0.7 cents and GPT-5.5 cost 1.4 cents. The newest reasoning models also write far more per answer than a fixed length allows, which pushes their real cost higher still.

All four cheap-line points are grade C, and two caveats are load-bearing. They use published list prices, not what it costs the seller to serve. And they hold the answer length fixed. Their remaining spread is not measurement error, because each is arithmetic on a printed price. It is the choice of which model counts as cheapest and capable.

With that said, the direction is stark. The floor cost of one machine-written answer is now roughly 0.6% of the ad revenue of one search query. In 2023 it was roughly 130% of it [e7-unit_econ-007].

Both ratios use the cheap line and the 3.7 cents of revenue per query. Price the top line instead, at GPT-5.4 Standard, and 0.6% becomes about 19%. Read the pair as a direction, not as a measurement.

## What the era hands on

Four things pass to the next chapter, and to the next project in this series.

No rival mechanism killed the auction. Display moved to first price for reasons of transparency, and gained no yield mechanism by doing so. Search kept its second-price auction, and Google fitted knobs to it. Same year, same firm, opposite directions. Tuning turned the mechanism into a price-setting instrument.

The audit chain broke. The seller now sells the space, runs the auction, counts the impression and grades its own homework. Every era before this one had someone else holding the tape measure.

The measured ad market shrank as a share of the economy while the firms that supposedly captured it grew. Either the market is smaller than the story says, or the story is measuring the wrong boundary. That question gets its own chapter.

And an answer now costs a rounding error against what a query earns. Whatever that funds, it is no longer blocked by the price of thinking.
