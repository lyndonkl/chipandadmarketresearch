# The Machine Market (2008-2026)

Era 7. Evidence freeze: 30 June 2026.

## The short version

In 2008 a person still bought most ads. By 2026 a machine bought them, and often wrote them too.

Three jobs moved into software in this era, in order. Buying went first. Then pricing. Then the ad itself. And the firm that ran the auction was usually the same firm that counted the result. That is the era's central fact. Most of its failures follow from it.

The era also killed the mechanism that era 6 was named for. Not by replacing it. By tuning it.

## The plumbing

Google closed its purchase of DoubleClick on 11 March 2008, for $3.1B [e7-events-001]. The price was not the point. The assets were. Google now owned the ad server that publishers used to decide which ad to show, plus the display plumbing that would become its exchange.

A year later that exchange relaunched with real-time bidding. Each single ad slot now went to auction as the page loaded. Buyers could bring their own data and their own models to each one. The industry wrote an open protocol for it soon after, so the method belonged to the market rather than to one firm.

It arrived in a bad year. US internet ad revenue fell 3% in 2009. That is the only fall in the whole era [e7-events-002].

"Programmatic" is just a name for this: buying ad slots by software and by rule, one at a time, instead of by phone call and insertion order. It won almost completely. By 2025, about 90% of all US non-search digital ad revenue was traded this way [e7-scale-005].

## The money and the medium

US internet ad revenue was $23,448M in 2008 [e7-scale-004]. In 2025 it was $294,593M. That is 12.6 times larger in seventeen years [e7-medium-001].

The 2025 split, by format: search $114.2B, display $81.6B, digital video $78.0B, digital audio $8.4B, and $12.5B in an "other" bucket that holds classifieds, directories and lead generation [e7-medium-004].

How much of all US ad money is now digital? Our best estimate is 74%, but the honest range is wide: somewhere between 72% and 82%, depending on whose universe you count [e7-medium-002]. The total itself is contested the same way. About $405B in 2025, with MAGNA at $398B and EMARKETER at $422B for what is supposed to be the same country and year [e7-scale-001]. Globally the gap is wider still: roughly $1.05T, with MAGNA at $979B and WPP Media at $1.14T [e7-scale-003].

Set that against the size of the economy and something odd appears. Measured US ad spend was about 1.32% of GDP in 2025 [e7-scale-002]. In 2007 the older Coen series read 2.0%. So the measured ad market shrank as a share of the economy while the platforms grew. That is a real puzzle, and this chapter does not settle it. Two warnings belong with the number. It is a grade C estimate. And the two years come from different series that do not count the same things, so part of the fall is a change in what "advertising" means.

One legend can be retired here. The high point for US ad spend as a share of GDP was not the year 2000. On the long series it falls in the early 1920s, and 2000 ranks well down the list. The dot-com peak is a story people tell, not a fact the data holds.

Older channels kept shrinking on schedule. Directory advertising, the Yellow Pages business, fell from a peak near $14.7B in 2005 to roughly $1.1B in 2024, though no audited series survives to pin it down; the honest range is $0.7B to $2.0B [e7-medium-006]. Direct mail was still worth about $37.3B in 2024, but USPS Marketing Mail volume fell from 99 billion pieces in 2008 to 56.8 billion in 2025 [e7-buyers-004]. The same money now buys far fewer, far better aimed pieces.

Two channels moved the other way. Connected TV reached about $33.35B in 2025 [e7-medium-005]. It is where brand television money finally crossed into an auction. Podcast revenue grew from $105.7M in 2015 to $2,862.2M in 2025 [e7-medium-007].

Whose money is it? No modern source publishes the split by money type, so this record rebuilds it. All four figures are grade C and the bands are wide. Every one of them is measured against MAGNA's $398B, which counts what media owners took in and leaves direct mail out, so direct mail is out of all four pools too. National direct response: about $151B, or 38%, and somewhere between $117B and $187B [e7-scale-009]. National brand money: about $146B, or 37%, and somewhere between $100B and $187B [e7-scale-006]. Local and retail buyers: about $94B, or 24%, and somewhere between $60B and $123B [e7-scale-007]. Classified and directory money: about $10B, or 2.5% [e7-scale-008]. The four run 0.6 points over the total, and we report that rather than shave it [e7-scale-010].

The top two cannot be ranked. Their centres sit 1.4 points apart and their ranges nearly coincide, and the three methods behind them do not even agree on which is bigger. What the record does say is that response money and brand money are now the two big pools, that local money is a clear third, and that classified is a distant fourth.

An earlier version of this chapter said brand money had become the smallest of the four. That was wrong, and the cause was a bookkeeping error rather than a judgement. The local figure it subtracted was BIA's $169B, which counts everyone who buys local audiences, national chains included. BIA's own split puts $116B of that with national and regional advertisers and only $59B with small businesses.

## Death one: header bidding

The auction died three times in this era. The first death was not Google's doing.

Publishers used to sell leftover ad space through a waterfall. The ad server called one exchange, offered the slot at a price, and moved to the next exchange only if the first declined. The order was fixed and the publisher set it. That order was worth a great deal to whoever sat at the top of it, because a buyer further down never saw what the buyer above had passed on.

Header bidding broke that. The publisher put a small piece of code in the page header. Every exchange bid at the same time, before the ad server was called. The trade press dates the break to 2014 and 2015, and an open-source project called Prebid.js turned the trick into a standard.

Two things ended at once. The waterfall's sequential price discrimination was over. And Google's ad server, which had enjoyed the first call, now had to win on price like everyone else.

## Deaths two and three: the 2019 fork

Then came 2019, and this is where most accounts go wrong.

Two changes landed that year. They ran in opposite directions, on opposite sides of the same company.

**Display went to first price.** On 5 September 2019 Google finished moving Ad Manager, its open-web display exchange, to a unified first-price auction [mech-first_price-001]. In a second-price auction the winner pays just enough to beat the runner-up. In a first-price auction the winner pays what it bid. Google's stated reason was a fair and transparent market under header-bidding pressure. It also folded publisher price floors into one set of rules at the same time. On revenue it reported only that testing showed a "neutral to positive" effect for publishers.

That modest claim is the correct one, and the mechanism explains why. A first-price rule is not a money machine. Buyers respond by shading: they bid below their true value, by an amount that depends on how many rivals they expect. Work the symmetric benchmark and the two rules pay the seller exactly the same, for any number of bidders. There is no yield mechanism in the first-price rule at all.

The popular version of this story quotes a frozen set of bids under both rules and reports a large windfall for first price. That reading is an artefact. It assumes bidders keep bidding the same numbers under a rule that punishes them for it. They do not.

The real change in 2019 was about who computes the clearing price. Under second price the seller computed it from the runner-up's bid. Under first price the buyer's own model computes it. That is the transparency argument, and it has force, because the party computing the old second price also ran the exchange and the publisher's ad server.

Calibration matters here. Revenue equivalence is a theorem about a benchmark, and the real display market broke it in at least three places. Buyers were not symmetric; they had different data and different shading models. The second price was computed by an interested party. And floors moved at the same time as the rule, so the two effects cannot be separated in publisher revenue data. The defensible claim is the negative one, and it is strong: nothing in the first-price rule mechanically raises yield.

**Search went the other way.** In the same year, Google launched rGSP on search. The letters stand for randomized generalized second-price. It shuffles the order of ads with similar scores, so a lower-ranked ad can sometimes appear above a higher-ranked one. That breaks the reliability of a trick sophisticated bidders had used: bidding down a slot to pay less. Break the trick, and bidders stop shading as hard.

The record on this is unusually good, because it came out in court. Pre-launch tests raised the top-slot cost per click by 5.91% on PCs and tablets, and 4.85% on mobile. About 40% to 50% of the gain was expected to stick. Two months after launch a 5.74% revenue gain was still there. Advertisers cannot opt out [e7-pricing-001, mech-rgsp-001]. The stated reason in the court's findings was revenue. Not transparency. Not fairness. Revenue.

**So: search never went first-price.** This is the most common factual error in retellings of 2019, and it is worth stating plainly. Any sentence that says "the ad market moved to first price in 2019" is a sentence about display. On search, the 2019 move went toward more seller control, not less.

## The knobs

The court record named three levers Google used on the search auction. Internally they were called "intentional pricing" [mech-knobs-001].

**Squashing**, launched under the name "Butternut Squash", compresses the quality term in the ranking formula. Era 6's auction ranked ads by bid times predicted click-through rate. Squashing raises that rate to a power below one, which flattens the quality dimension. The winner can stay the same while its price rises toward the runner-up's raw bid. Push the dial hard enough and the ranking flips back to the crude pure-bid order, which is worse for the searcher and better for the seller on the same impression. The exponent has never been disclosed, so no outside party can reproduce its effect.

**Format pricing** charges more for the shape of an ad rather than for its position. It had grown to about 20% of Google's search text-ads revenue per thousand queries before rGSP replaced it. Individual experiments launched at about +15% and kept roughly half of that for the long term [e7-pricing-002, mech-format_pricing-001]. A share that large is what a handful of launches of the disclosed size compound to. None of it touches ranking, ad copy or relevance.

**rGSP** is the third, described above.

On top of the three sat routine tuning. Google's VP of ads testified that the firm moved auction and reserve prices to hit quarterly revenue targets. The moves ran about 5% on average, and up to 10% on some queries. Advertisers were not told [e7-pricing-003, mech-tuning-001].

Two points make sense of this, and both cut against the tidy story of a good auction gone bad.

First, it is not new. From August 2005 Google set minimum bids per keyword using its own quality score [mech-quality_score-001]. On a query with one bidder, the minimum bid is the price. Moving it is a direct price decision by the seller, with no rival involved. The seller has been posting prices on the long tail since the middle of era 6.

Second, the knobs did not have to be large. The old second-price auction already left the seller a wide band, because what an advertiser pays depends on how well it plays, and most play badly. The seller sits on that band. A 5% or 6% nudge moves the market a short way inside it. The mechanism did not have to be abandoned to become a price-setting instrument. It only had to be tuned, and the tuning was not disclosed.

That leaves the governance point, which is the same in 2005 and in 2019. Every number that decides who wins and what they pay was computed by the seller: the predicted click-through rate, the quality reserve, the squashing exponent, the format multiplier, the test for whether a click was real. None was published. None was audited. The first industry standard for counting a click did not arrive until 2009 [mech-audit-001]. The design case for the auction is sound. The governance case never existed.

## What the middle keeps

If software buys the ad, software also takes a cut at every hop. Three studies, three angles, one answer.

A UK supply-chain study traced advertiser money through to publishers. Only 51% arrived. A further 15% was an "unknown delta" that could not be attributed to any named party in the chain. Only 12% of impressions could be followed end to end [e7-pricing-004].

A US study of the open web traced money through the demand-side platform, the software a buyer uses to bid for ad slots. About 36 cents of each dollar entering one reached a consumer. It put roughly $22B of recoverable waste in an $88B pool, and found that made-for-advertising sites, which exist to carry ads rather than readers, absorbed about 15% of spend [e7-pricing-005].

Google published its own figure in June 2020: publishers keep about 69 cents of an advertiser dollar when both ends of the trade sit inside Google. The DOJ's number for the same market is different. It says Google keeps at least 35 cents of every open-web display dollar, and that its exchange has charged a 20% take rate since 2009 [e7-pricing-006].

## The signals get cut

Targeting in this era ran a full arc: from the finest-grained tracking ever built, through two deliberate cuts, to something coarser and far more powerful.

GDPR took effect in May 2018 and required consent for the data collection that behavioural targeting ran on. The measured effects are real, modest and contested. One large-publisher study found click-through down 2.1% and revenue per click down 5.7%. Another found roughly 10% falls in recorded page views and revenue for EU users. A publisher survey found no aggregate fall at all [e7-targeting-003]. Keep the disagreement; it is the finding.

Apple's App Tracking Transparency shipped with iOS 14.5 in April 2021. Apps now had to ask before tracking users across other firms' apps and sites. Opt-in ran about 16% at launch and rose to roughly 25% by mid-2022 [e7-targeting-002]. Meta told investors the change would cost it "on the order of $10 billion" in 2022, and outside estimates ran as high as $12.8B [e7-targeting-001]. Note what this was. Not a law. A platform rule, written by one firm, that cost the largest buyer of mobile signal more than any privacy statute had.

The third shock never landed. Google announced in January 2020 that it would retire the third-party cookie within two years. It delayed three times. In July 2024 it said it would not deprecate the cookie unilaterally, and in April 2025 it dropped even the choice prompt. The cookie outlived the decade of preparation for its funeral. That non-event is the honest finding, and it matters for what comes next.

## Nobody counts the counter

For a century the market's discipline was simple. A third party counted the audience, and the buyer could audit someone other than the seller. That rule broke here.

Facebook overstated average video view time for about two years by excluding views under three seconds. Plaintiffs said the method inflated the number by up to 900%. It paid $40M in October 2019 to settle the advertiser class action [e7-measurement-001].

The counting institutions took damage from the other side too. The Media Rating Council suspended Nielsen's national and local TV accreditation in September 2021, after finding pandemic-era undercounts of 2% to 6% among adults under 50. A broadcaster trade body put the cost to networks at $468M or more. National accreditation came back in April 2023, nineteen months later [e7-measurement-002].

Programmatic added a counting crisis of its own. By 2026 the IAB reported that bots make up over half of global web traffic, with nearly 40% of it malicious [e7-measurement-003]. The same body estimates that better measurement could recover more than $26B of US media money now going to the wrong places [e7-measurement-005]. That is a direct price tag on the failure.

Then privacy law removed the signals attribution ran on, and buyers retreated to older, coarser tools. Media mix models infer what worked from spending and sales history rather than from tracking one person. Holdout tests keep the ads from part of the audience and compare. That is a regime change in how the market knows what worked, not a change of software.

Who pays the counter is the era's quiet answer. Increasingly the seller, or nobody.

## Two sellers, then three

The seller side concentrated, then re-concentrated around a new pole.

The top ten sellers took 84.1% of US internet ad revenue in 2025, up from 78.6% in 2021 [e7-sellers-001]. In absolute terms, Google's ad revenue was $294,691M in 2025 [e7-sellers-002]. Meta's was $196.18B and Amazon's ad services line was $68.63B [e7-sellers-003].

Now a result that looks backwards until you read it twice. Google, Meta and Amazon together took about 71.9% of US digital ad spend in 2025, down from 73.9% in 2020. Over the same years their share of all US ad spend rose from about 47% to about 59% [e7-sellers-004]. Concentration inside digital eased slightly. Concentration of the whole market rose sharply. Digital was eating everything else faster than anyone was eating Google.

Amazon is the structural novelty. US retail media reached $60.32B in 2025, and Amazon took more than three quarters of it [e7-sellers-007]. Retail media turns a shop into a media owner selling its own shelf. But the money is mostly not new. It is trade-promotion and co-op money, the manufacturer money that has flowed through retailers for a century. What changed is the pricing mechanism. A negotiated allowance became an auction.

Prices were rising as the era closed. Google's cost per click rose 7% in 2025 while paid clicks rose 6% [e7-pricing-007]. Meta's average price per ad rose 9% while impressions rose 12% [e7-pricing-008]. Supply and price rose together on both platforms. In a market with easy entry, that combination is unusual.

## The courts arrive

On 5 August 2024 Judge Mehta held that Google had illegally monopolised general search and general search text advertising. The court found Google held 89.2% of US general search queries in 2020, and 88% of the search text ad market [e7-sellers-005].

The instrument was distribution, not the auction. Google paid $26.3B in search default revenue share in 2021 to be the box people type into [e7-events-009]. It paid Apple $20B in 2022 under one agreement [mech-default-002], on terms reported at about 36% of the search ad revenue Safari generated [mech-default-003]. The challenged contracts covered roughly half of all US general search queries [mech-mehta-001]. Google's own 2017 estimate was that defaults drove 54% of its search revenue [mech-mehta-002]. And it had tested a 5% text-ad price rise and kept it, without losing advertisers of any consequence [mech-mehta-004]. That last fact is what monopoly power looks like as a number.

The remedy came on 2 September 2025. Default payments survived. Exclusivity did not, and the court ordered data sharing with qualified rivals. There was no order to sell Chrome [mech-mehta-005]. The judge wrote that generative AI had changed the course of the case.

A second court reached a second verdict. On 17 April 2025 Judge Brinkema held that Google had monopolised the open-web publisher ad server and ad exchange markets, and had unlawfully tied them together. No remedies order had issued by this record's freeze date. It is an open item, not a result.

Meanwhile the distribution engine kept growing and changed shape. Google paid $59,926M in traffic acquisition cost in 2025, a rate of 20.3% of ad revenue [e7-sellers-006]. In era 6 most of that money bought syndicated inventory. By 2021 the majority of it, on our best estimate around 58%, went on search defaults instead [mech-default-004]. That figure is grade C. The direction is not in doubt. Google stopped mainly paying for places to put ads, and started mainly paying to be found.

## The machines start answering

Google launched AI Overviews in May 2024, putting a generated answer above the results. By July 2025 the format had more than 2 billion monthly users, and Google said it earns at about the same rate as classic search [e7-targeting-004].

The cost shows up on the other side of the page. Pew's browsing panel found users clicked a search result on 8% of visits where an AI summary appeared, against 15% of visits without one. They clicked a source cited inside the summary on 1% of visits [e7-measurement-004]. Google disputes the method, and this is a single panel study, so treat it as a signal rather than a settled rate. If it holds, the summary keeps the reader and the open web loses the click.

On the buy side, the advertiser stopped choosing. Google's Performance Max and Meta's Advantage+ take a budget, a goal and a feed of assets, and pick the audience, the placement and the creative themselves. Meta disclosed that its automated ad products had passed a $60B annualised revenue rate [e7-targeting-005]. Adoption of Google's version is harder to pin down: a vendor survey put it at 71% of advertisers, but the population figure is not published, so our honest range is somewhere between 50% and 85% [e7-buyers-003].

The machine also writes the ad now. Within a month of launching its generative creative tools, Meta said more than a million advertisers had used them to make over 15 million ads [e7-creators-004].

The era closes with targeting moving inside the conversation. Meta began using Meta AI chat interactions for ad targeting in December 2025, with no opt-out. OpenAI's ChatGPT ad pilot went live in February 2026 for US adults on its free and cheapest tiers. Perplexity went the other way, abandoning the sponsored-question product it had launched a year earlier. Two of the three bets are still open at the freeze.

## Who makes the ads now

The people who make ads split three ways in this era.

The holding companies kept merging and kept losing the media-buying job. Omnicom closed its purchase of Interpublic in November 2025, forming a group with combined revenue above $25B [e7-creators-001]. Meanwhile the work left. By 2023, 82% of ANA member marketers had an in-house agency, up from 78% in 2018 [e7-creators-002]. The 15% commission that the first agencies settled on in the 1890s finished dying here [e1-pricing-002]. Its successor is principal media: the agency buys inventory in bulk and resells it to the client at a markup it does not disclose.

The platforms became creators, as the last section showed. And individual creators became a channel of their own. US creator advertising was worth about $37B in 2025, with about $44B projected for 2026, growing at roughly twice the rate of the market [e7-creators-003].

The buyer base widened past anything the old market could serve. Meta has cited more than 10 million active advertisers [e7-buyers-001]. Political money became a cyclical shock of its own, at $11.1B across the 2023-24 US cycle [e7-buyers-002].

## What a query is worth

Era 7 is the first era where we can price the unit itself, and the unit is a search query.

Our best estimate is that Google earned about 3.7 cents of ad revenue per query in 2025, with an honest range of roughly 3 to 5 cents [e7-unit_econ-001]. It is grade C, because the revenue line includes some non-search products and the query count comes from a company statement, not an audit.

What did the query cost? The court record gives us 2020: about 0.65 cents to run the search engine and the search ads business, per query, with a range of 0.4 to 1 cent [e7-unit_econ-002]. Now put the distribution number beside it. The default payments the court examined worked out to roughly 0.8 cents per query. Google paid more per query to be found than it spent answering it. That single comparison explains the antitrust case better than any market-share figure.

The margin, at the segment level, is not in dispute. Google Services earned a 40.7% operating margin in 2025, after paying $59.9B of traffic acquisition cost [e7-unit_econ-003].

Now the comparison this project was built to make. What does it cost a machine to write one answer?

Fix the answer at a standard size, roughly a page of input and a paragraph of output, then price it at the cheapest capable model of each year. Every point below carries a wide band, because which model serves the answer matters more than which year it is.

In 2023 it cost about 4.8 cents, on a range of 3 to 6 cents [e7-unit_econ-004]. That is roughly the entire ad revenue of a search query. By July 2024 the same answer cost about 0.033 cents, on a range of 0.025 to 0.95 cents [e7-unit_econ-005]. By 2025 it was about 0.022 cents, on a range of 0.015 to 0.43 cents [e7-unit_econ-006]. At the freeze in mid-2026 it was still about 0.022 cents, on a range of 0.015 to 0.6 cents. The cheap tier had stopped falling [e7-unit_econ-007].

The twelve-month fall from 2023 to 2024 is about 145 times at the middle of each band. That multiple is not measured. Compare the top of each band and the fall is about six times. Compare the bottom of each and it is about 120 times. The fall is real at any pairing. The size of it is a choice about which tier you price.

All four are grade C, and the caveats are load-bearing. They use published list prices, not what it costs the seller to serve. They hold the answer length fixed. That last assumption is now the weak point, because reasoning models write far more per answer, which pushes real frontier cost back up by a large multiple.

With that said, the direction is stark. The floor cost of one machine-written answer is now roughly 0.6% of the ad revenue of one search query. In 2023 it was roughly 130% of it [e7-unit_econ-007].

Both ratios use the cheap tier and the 3.7 cents of revenue per query. Price the frontier tier instead, at the top of each cost band, and 0.6% becomes about 16% while 130% becomes about 162%. Read the pair as a direction, not as a measurement.

## What the era hands on

Four things pass to the next chapter, and to P3.

The auction did not die by being replaced. Display moved to first price for reasons of transparency, and gained no yield mechanism by doing so. Search kept its second-price auction and had knobs fitted to it. Same year, same firm, opposite directions. The mechanism became a price-setting instrument by being tuned.

The audit chain broke. The seller now sells the space, runs the auction, counts the impression and grades its own homework. Every era before this one had someone else holding the tape measure.

The measured ad market shrank as a share of the economy while the firms that supposedly captured it grew. Either the market is smaller than the story says, or the story is measuring the wrong boundary. That question gets its own chapter.

And an answer now costs a rounding error against what a query earns. Whatever that funds, it is no longer blocked by the price of thinking.
