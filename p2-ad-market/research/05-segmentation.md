# Segmentation, 1976-1993

The market before this one sold a big audience all at once. Three networks, a few magazines, the town paper. A buyer paid a price for each thousand people reached, and took whoever showed up.

Cable channels multiplied, and the census went on sale sorted by postal code. Between 1976 and 1993 the market learned to sell slices. Sellers cut the audience into pieces and priced each piece on its own. A cable channel held one kind of viewer. A ZIP code held one kind of household. Buyers stopped paying for a nation and started paying for a niche.

That is the mechanism of this era. Here is what it was not. Nobody could buy a person's intent yet. They could buy the mailbox on a chosen block, and the channel a chosen sort of household watched. Every gain in aim came from sorting people by where they lived or what they tuned to. Never from knowing what they wanted right now.

## The money

Measured US ad spend was $33.3 billion in 1976. By 1993 it was $141.0 billion. That is 4.2 times more money in seventeen years, an 8.9% rise each year.

"Measured" is carrying weight in that sentence. The totals count paid space and time. They do not count coupons, or the money a brand pays a supermarket for shelf space and features. We come back to that gap at the end, because it is large.

Against the size of the economy, ads rose from about 1.8% of GDP in 1976 to a flat 2.3% in the years 1986 to 1989, then eased to about 2.1% by 1993. This era is the climb, not the peak. Different versions of these figures put the top after 1960 in 2000, between 2.3% and 2.5%. That gap is real and we leave it open rather than pick a winner. The whole-series high is older and higher: about 3.0% of GDP in 1922.

One year in this era breaks the trend. Spend fell 1.24% in 1991, the first drop in plain dollar terms since 1961. Mail, directories and cable all kept growing straight through it, which is this era's story told in a single bad year.

We have one world figure, and it is soft. World spend across the five main media was about $202 billion in 1990. Our honest range is $180 billion to $230 billion. Counting mail and sales promotion, the same forecast said about $267 billion. The US was near 39% of the five-media number. The source page now sits behind a login, and we recovered the figures from a search index, so treat the number as a marker of size and not much more.

One more caution before any chart. The main series here was built by one man, Robert Coen at the agency McCann-Erickson. It prices space at the seller's list price, not at what buyers paid. Even its own vintages disagree. The 1990 total is printed as $129,590 million in one edition and $129,968 million in another, a gap of 0.29%. Small, but it tells you the series moves.

## The ranking most people misremember

Newspapers fell from 28.9% of measured spend in 1976 to 22.7% in 1993, and still tripled in dollars. They were still the largest single medium in 1993, at $32.0 billion against broadcast television's $28.0 billion. Television only passed them in 1992, and only when you add cable to broadcast: $31.1 billion against $30.7 billion.

Radio held 6.7% of spend in 1993, against 7.0% in 1976. It drifted, and it was never near the top of the table. The story that radio once led American media money does not survive contact with the spend data, in this era or any other.

Cable is the headline of the era and the smallest number in this list. Cable ad money grew from $72 million in 1980 to $4.45 billion in 1993. That is 62 times more in thirteen years, and still only 3.2% of the market.

The two mediums that actually grew into the gap barely feature in most tellings. Direct mail went from $4.79 billion in 1976 to $27.27 billion in 1993, third biggest in the country. Yellow Pages went from $2.9 billion in 1980 to $9.52 billion in 1993. That beat radio at $9.46 billion and magazines at $7.36 billion.

## Cable split the audience but not the money

The three networks stopped being the market. Their share of the prime-time audience fell from about 90% in the mid-1970s to somewhere between 61% and 70% by the 1988-89 season, while cable reached 20%. Our two sources disagree on the low end, so we carry both figures rather than settle it. The direction is not in doubt.

Supply exploded. The count of national cable networks went from 28 in 1980 to 79 in 1990. Channels arrived built around sport, music, news, children, women. A buyer no longer chose a time of day. A buyer chose a kind of person, nationally.

But cable was a poor seller of advertising, and the reason is structural. Ads supplied only 9.8% of US cable industry revenue in 1993, $2.6 billion out of $26.9 billion. Basic subscriptions alone supplied 50.6%. Cable's real customer was the household paying a monthly bill, not the advertiser. The medium that broke the mass audience had little reason to build a strong ad market on top of the pieces. It had already been paid.

## The census becomes a targeting tool

The other half of segmentation happened in the mail, and it started with public data.

Jonathan Robbin's firm Claritas took the 1970 census, moved the counts off the census map and onto all roughly 36,000 five-digit ZIP codes, and sorted those ZIPs into 40 named types. The system was PRIZM. Sources disagree on when it went on sale: the strongest one says 1974, and later accounts say 1976 or 1978. We carry 1974 and let the range run to 1978.

The idea is simple and it was new. People who live alike buy alike. A mailer who knows nothing about you can guess a great deal from your block. Donnelley and CACI sold rival cluster systems within a few years, and the Postal Service later cut its codes into far smaller delivery units, which pushed the buyable unit below the ZIP and down toward the street.

Then the mail arrived. Bulk advertising mail, sent at the third-class postal rate, rose from 30.4 billion pieces in 1980 to 65.8 billion in 1993. That moved from 28.6% to 38.4% of everything the Postal Service delivered. Per person that is 253 pieces a year by 1993, against 133 in 1980, or roughly 660 a year for an average household.

Here is the ceiling on all of it. Renting a list of names and getting a 2% reply counted as a good campaign; 2% to 3% counted as strong. The best-aimed medium of the era missed 98 people in every 100.

## The quiet giant

Direct mail was the third largest medium in the country for this whole era, and it rose every single year, including through the 1991 slump. It went from 14.4% of measured spend in 1976 to 19.3% in 1993.

The more striking fact is about national money. Direct mail took 33.3% of all US national ad spend in 1993, $27.3 billion out of $81.9 billion. That is more than every national television line added together: the networks, spot buys across local stations, shows sold direct to stations, and cable channels came to $22.9 billion. The largest national medium in America in 1993 was the mailbox.

Two honest caveats sit on that comparison, and both cut the same way. The series books all direct mail as national, which flatters the share. And the direct-mail line includes postage, printing, lists and creative work, while a television line is just the price of the time. Postage alone was about 36% of the direct-mail figure in 1993, when we set postal revenue against that line. A dollar of mail and a dollar of network TV are not the same kind of dollar. A later rebuild of the same history, done from what media owners actually earned, puts 1980 direct mail near 12% where this series puts it at 14.2%. We show both.

The mechanics under the giant were plain arithmetic. Renting a thousand names cost $50 to $80 if those people had responded to something before, and $25 to $30 for a list merely compiled from public records. Printing and preparing a piece cost at least 40 cents. Postage per piece rose from 7.9 cents in 1980 to 14.9 cents in 1993, an 88% rise, and we got that by dividing postal revenue by pieces rather than by reading a published rate.

The buyer's sum was cost per order, not cost per thousand eyes. That is an older tradition than television, and it never stopped running.

The Postal Service was, on these numbers, one of the largest sellers in the American ad economy. Third-class revenue went from $2.4 billion in 1980 to $9.8 billion in 1993. A production industry grew beside it: firms doing direct-mail work took $8.3 billion of receipts in 1993, against $3.8 billion in 1985, with 3,900 places of business and 81,000 staff.

Adding up all response-driven money is harder, and we mark it as our weakest estimate here. Catalogers, publishers, insurers, card issuers, fundraisers and the new infomercial sellers spent an estimated $31 billion in 1993, about 22% of measured spend. That is grade C work. The honest range is $28 billion to $36 billion, because nobody in the era published a single total across media.

## Two more giants nobody counts

America's phone monopoly broke up in 1984, and the directories went to the regional phone companies. Yellow Pages sales grew to $9.5 billion by 1993, and 87.1% of that was local money, sold door to door on yearly contracts. A plumber paid for size and position in a category that customers were already flipping to. That is the nearest ancestor this era had to paid search, and it was bigger than radio.

Classified was the other one. Individuals and small firms bought it by the line, served themselves, and used no agency at all. It grew from $2.6 billion in 1976 to $11.2 billion in 1993: 35% of all newspaper ad revenue and 7.9% of measured US spend. It carried on climbing to a peak of $19.6 billion in 2000, and then fell 71% by 2010.

Notice where local money sat. Local buyers were 41.9% of measured spend in 1993, down from 44.9% in 1976, while national buyers rose from 55.1% to 58.1%. In dollars that is $59.1 billion local against $81.9 billion national. But the local share of single mediums was extreme: 88.7% of newspaper ad revenue and 87.1% of Yellow Pages revenue. Local and self-serve money was packed into exactly the mediums the web would empty first.

## The agency business changes owner

The people making the ads had a strange decade. The trade grew and concentrated at the same time.

Agency billings, meaning what clients spent through agencies, rose from $15.45 billion in 1977 to $69.59 billion in 1992. Agency income, meaning what the agencies kept, rose from $3.17 billion to $13.61 billion. The count of agency offices with staff rose from 8,089 in 1977 to 13,879 in 1992, about 70% more places of business. Staff numbers fell late: about 137,000 in 1990 down to 127,000 in 1992, though a second census count for the same year says 132,000. We leave that gap open.

Ownership moved the other way. Four listed holding companies bought the industry's best-known names. BBDO, Doyle Dane Bernbach and Needham Harper merged into Omnicom in 1986. Saatchi & Saatchi bought Ted Bates that same year for about $450 million, creating a group with over $7.2 billion of billings. WPP took J. Walter Thompson in 1987 for $566 million, the first hostile takeover the ad trade had seen work. WPP then took Ogilvy in 1989 for $864 million, the most ever paid for an agency at that point.

Fewer owners at the top, more shops at the bottom. Media buying specialists, creative boutiques and direct-marketing firms multiplied under the holding companies. The holding company did not own a creative style. It owned a set of client relationships and a media buying block big enough to argue with sellers.

## The 15% did not die here

The received story is that the 15% commission died in the 1980s. The commission worked like this: the agency billed the client the full media price, and kept a slice of it, with the media owner absorbing the cut. It made the agency's pay a fixed share of what the client spent.

The Economic Census, which asks agencies where their money came from, says the rate held. Commissions actually received on media billings were 14.13% in 1977, 13.92% in 1982, 14.16% in 1987 and 13.18% in 1992. The collapse comes after this era: 10.98% by 1997. The census is grade A data, and it kills the legend cleanly.

What did move was the mix, in two ways. First, fewer big advertisers used commissions at all. Tracking studies put 71% of large national advertisers on commissions in 1982 and 61% by 1994, while paying agencies a fee for labour rose from 8% to 31%. Second, commissions shrank as a share of what agencies earned: 58.6% of agency income in 1977, 61.3% in 1987, then 54.2% in 1992, with service fees at 24.6%.

The pressure came from price. Media prices rose about 27% faster than prices in general between 1977 and 1992, when we compared two published indexes. The fair range is 20% to 35%. A fixed percentage of a fast-rising base pays the agency more each year for the same work. Clients noticed. The commission bent in this era and broke in the next one.

## The year the instrument changed the price

In September 1987 Nielsen switched the count that priced national television to the people meter, a box on which each viewer pressed a button, replacing paper diaries and meters that only knew the set was on.

Measured prime-time viewing fell about 10% at once. CBS and ABC each lost 13%, NBC 4%. The Saturday morning children's audience fell by at least 20% and possibly 50%, against roughly $150 million of children's ad revenue across the three networks. Networks owed make-goods, meaning free replacement airtime, and the replacement time was scarcer and more expensive than what they had sold.

Nothing about the audience changed that September. Only the instrument did. The networks attacked the panel rather than the result. They said 33% of metered homes took pay cable, against 26% of the country. Those two shares do not measure the same thing. One counts homes and the other counts people, and cable homes were larger than average. Nielsen answered in the same article that the panel ran about 31% pay cable against 28% to 30% nationally, which cuts the gap from seven points to one or three. Read the 33 against 26 as a charge the networks made, not as a measured skew. That fight is the clearest proof this market ever produced that the counting method sets the price.

Response advertisers were counting a different way, and had been for decades: keyed coupons, source codes, toll-free numbers. From 1979 a new machine tied the two halves together. IRI's BehaviorScan wired two small towns, put scanners in 15 supermarkets, recruited 2,000 households in each town, and cut test commercials into those same homes' televisions. It is the first electronic link from a specific ad exposure to a specific purchase. The idea that tying an ad to a sale was a digital invention does not survive this record. It was built in two small towns, on cable and supermarket scanners, before the web existed.

## What the totals leave out

The largest shift in brand money in the 1980s does not appear in any number above.

Trade promotion, meaning the money a brand pays retailers for discounts, displays and shelf position, took 44.9% of packaged-goods marketing budgets by 1992, overtaking media advertising during the decade. None of it is in the measured-media totals. A chart of this era showing brands shifting between mediums tells you a partial truth. Some of the money was not moving between mediums. It was leaving advertising for the retailer.

## What this era hands on

By 1993 the market had a fine-grained but blunt set of tools. It could buy a neighbourhood type, a mail route, a channel with a known kind of viewer. It could measure a reply exactly and an audience only through a panel that could be argued with. It could not buy a person's stated want.

It also had a large pile of money already being spent against response rather than reach: about $11.2 billion of classified, $9.5 billion of directories, $27.3 billion of mail. That money was self-serve, local, small-business, and priced by result. It was the least glamorous part of the market and the most exposed. When a machine finally arrived that could take a stated want and sell it by auction, that is the money it took first.
