# Sponsorship, 1918-1949

## The deal

Radio was new. The deal was not.

A network sold a sponsor one hour of air time, and almost nothing else. The sponsor's agency did the rest. It wrote the show, hired the star, booked the band, settled the performers' contracts, and sent a finished program down the wire for the network to broadcast.

The FCC, the federal body that licensed broadcasters, put this on the record in 1941. It found that "it is frequently neither the station nor the network, but rather the advertising agency, which determines what broadcast programs shall contain."

That is the era in two lines. Networks sold time. Advertisers owned shows.

The agency earned its money the old way, the way the last era had settled: 15 percent of what its client spent on media. On radio that same 15 percent bought the program as well [e2-creators-002]. The agency earned its cut on the time and threw in the show.

The show looked free. It was not. We estimate talent and production took about 35 percent of what a sponsor spent on network radio. Nobody audited sponsor program budgets, so the honest range is wide. The true share sits somewhere between 20 and 55 percent [e2-pricing-008].

Agency income grew with the shows. Sources put Ayer's gross receipts at $13.7 million in 1919 and $38.0 million in 1930 [e2-creators-003]. J. Walter Thompson, a New York agency, led the trade in billings from 1922 to 1972, and sources put its billings late in the 1920s near $37.5 million [e2-creators-004]. Hold both dollar figures loosely. Nobody on this project checked either figure against its source, so both carry our weakest grade, C.

## The rate card

AT&T's station WEAF made the first sale of air time anyone documented, on 28 August 1922. The Queensboro Corporation paid $50 for ten minutes; one account says $100 for fifteen [e2-pricing-001]. AT&T's idea was that a broadcaster should rent time the way a phone company rents a line. By the end of 1925 it was renting a basic 13-station hookup at $2,600 an hour [e2-pricing-002]. RCA then bought AT&T out of the business for $1 million in November 1926 [e2-events-001] and built NBC on what it bought.

The networks turned rented time into a printed price list. Each station on the CBS card had an evening-hour rate. The rates ran from $125 up to $1,250 [e2-pricing-003]. Every other slot was a fixed share of the evening rate. A daytime hour cost exactly half. A Sunday afternoon hour cost two thirds or three quarters. An hour after midnight cost a third [e2-pricing-004].

Big buyers got a discount. It started at 2.5 percent and rose to 25 percent for a firm billing $1.2 million a year [e2-pricing-005]. NBC ran two networks, called Red and Blue. It counted the billings of both together when it worked out the discount, which is exactly what the FCC later seized on to argue the two were really one business.

Mutual, the third network, had no card of its own. It billed at its stations' rates and kept 3.5 percent of what member stations earned from network work [e2-pricing-006].

Now follow $100 of network billing in 1938. About $15 went to the agency. About $62 stayed with the network. About $23 reached the stations that put the show in the air [e2-sellers-005]. That split is our arithmetic on audited FCC totals, not an audited number in itself, so we hold it loosely: the network's share could be $58 or $66.

Stations were paid on a step scale. Under NBC's formula the first 16 hours in each 28-day period paid the station nothing. The next block paid 20 percent of the average hourly rate, then 30 percent, then 37.5 percent [e2-pricing-007]. Over time the stations pried more out of the deal. NBC paid out 20.5 percent of its network time sales in 1928 and 28.5 percent in 1940, while those sales grew from $7.3 million to $37.1 million [e2-sellers-007].

## Who owned the air

The FCC audited the whole business and published the numbers in 1941. They are the best figures the era produced.

In 1938 the US broadcast industry sold $100,892,259 of air time, counted after discounts and agency cuts had come out. The NBC and CBS networks took 44 percent of that, and the 23 stations they owned took 7 percent more [e2-sellers-001]. Two firms handled over half the country's radio business.

660 commercial stations were on air at the end of that year, and 341 of them were tied to a national network [e2-sellers-003]. The networks kept 73 percent of network time sales and paid the other 27 percent to the 253 stations that carried the shows [e2-sellers-002]. Counting networks and owned stations together, NBC and CBS earned about 49 percent of the industry's operating income [e2-sellers-004]. That last figure is ours, worked out from two audited FCC lines, so we grade it C.

Reach was just as lopsided. The FCC published that stations tied to NBC or CBS held "over 85 percent" of the transmitting power that carried signals across the country after dark. Our own read of the table behind that line gives about 92 percent, and the gap turns on which stations you count [e2-events-003]. Only the FCC's own careful phrase is solid.

Washington broke the top of that structure. The FCC's chain-broadcasting rules forced RCA to sell one of its two networks. Edward Noble paid $8 million for NBC Blue in October 1943 and renamed it the American Broadcasting Company [e2-events-006]. Antitrust made the third network, not technology.

Then the seller base exploded. About 900 AM stations were on air in 1945, and roughly 2,500 by the early 1950s [e2-sellers-006]. More sellers meant more time for sale, and the networks lost their grip on the price.

## Counting the crowd

A paper could count copies, and since the last era an outside body had audited that count. Radio had nothing to count. Nobody knew who was listening, so nobody knew what an hour was worth.

The Cooperative Analysis of Broadcasting, or CAB, filled the hole. It began field work in March 1930. Interviewers phoned homes and asked what people had heard the day before. Each report covered 17,000 households that owned a radio, across 50 cities [e2-events-002]. In its first year the CAB sold that work to 49 advertisers at $70 a month, or $60 for members of the advertisers' association, and took in $33,045 [e2-measurement-001].

Note who paid. Buyers paid, and they shut the sellers out as subscribers until 1937. The largest buyer of audience even chaired the board that ran the count. Broadcasting became priceable the moment it became countable, and the buyers made sure they held the counter.

The method had a hole in it. In 1934-35 only 31 percent of US families had a phone, while 96 percent of phone homes had a radio [e2-measurement-002]. A phone survey therefore leaned toward the well-off. Every rating of the era carried that tilt.

Then a rival medium bought its own audit. In the autumn of 1934 Clark-Hooper began calling homes during the show itself, in 16 cities, paid for by magazine publishers who thought the CAB was flattering radio [e2-measurement-004]. That is the cleanest proof in this whole history that the funder of a count shapes the count.

At first it went the funders' way. The first Clark-Hooper ratings came in below the CAB's for most shows, which is what the publishers had paid to see. The blow landed ten years later, and from the other side. In 1944 the CAB gave in and adopted Hooper's own method, so the two services were now asking the same question the same way. Their answers should have converged. Instead Hooper's numbers still ran about 20 percent above the CAB's [e2-measurement-003]. Hans Zeisel, at the agency McCann-Erickson, traced the gap to how each service worked the phones: how many rings before hanging up, how busy signals were handled, what to do with a listener who could not name the show. Same method, different answers. That is what made the finding fatal. Once McCann-Erickson showed that in early 1946, the industry's own service collapsed. Hooper took over its accounts that July, and buyer-run counting was over. Vendors sold the count to both sides from then on.

A research firm called A. C. Nielsen industrialised the job with a meter wired into the set. The Nielsen Radio Index went commercial in December 1942 with 800 metered homes and had 47 subscribers by 1945 [e2-measurement-005]. Every one of those homes sat in the east central states. That region held about a quarter of US households, and the quarter is the size of the region, not the reach of the sample. It was a ceiling, not an achievement, and it is why the service sold slowly. Nielsen said the meter had cost nearly ten years and about $2 million to get right [e2-measurement-007]. He said so in a sales speech in March 1946, when the service still reached only 63 percent of US homes and could not yet stand for the country. By April 1949 Nielsen could draw its sample from 97 percent of US homes, so for the first time its ratings could stand for the whole country [e2-measurement-006].

By then a rating was a contract term, not a report. In January 1949 CBS took the comedian Jack Benny from NBC by promising his sponsor $3,000 a week for every rating point his show lost in the move [e2-measurement-008]. One point meant one percent of the homes Hooper counted. Attention now had a published unit price.

One more shift happened quietly. Agencies, not advertisers, became the main buyer of the count. By the CAB's fourth year 27 agencies subscribed against 17 advertisers [e2-buyers-003]. Say the other half out loud. Advertiser subscribers fell from 49 in the first year to 21 in the third and 17 in the fourth. That is a drop of 65 percent, while agencies went from none to a majority [e2-measurement-001]. The middleman had become the market's information buyer.

## The daypart was the target

Nobody could target a person in this era. They targeted a slot.

Start from the price rule. A daytime hour cost exactly half an evening hour, and the daytime audience was mostly women at home. A buyer who wanted homemakers therefore bought mornings and afternoons, at half price. That one rule built the daytime serial. Procter and Gamble and its agencies filled the cheap hours with drama aimed at the people who bought soap, which is why we still say soap opera.

Print did the same trick by title. Women's magazines took about 39 percent of all US magazine ad money in 1935 [e2-targeting-001]. That figure is ours and it is soft: the source sorts magazines by subject matter, not by who read them.

Advertisers bought geography station by station, through firms that sold time for stations they did not own. This is spot buying, and it grew fast. National spot radio went from $15 million in 1935 to $123 million in 1949 [e2-targeting-002]. Local spot reached $245 million in 1949, passing network radio at $203 million [e2-targeting-003]. Inside radio, the network share of dollars fell from 55.8 percent in 1935 to 35.6 percent in 1949 [e2-buyers-001]. Sponsorship was still the era's signature, but the money was already leaking out of it.

The base kept growing. 83 percent of US homes had a radio set by 1940, up from 40.3 percent in 1930, and about 95 percent by 1950 [e2-targeting-005].

Counting set the ceiling on all of it. A daypart is a block of the broadcast day, like morning or evening. The CAB asked about one block a day at first, four in 1935, eight in 1940, and ran 32 sets of interviews a day by 1942 [e2-targeting-004]. That is as fine as the slicing got. The smallest thing anyone could buy was a daypart, in a market, for a class of home. Nothing narrower was for sale, because nothing narrower could be counted.

## What the money actually did

Now the part that argues with the chapter title.

Radio was the era's new machine, and it was never the biggest medium. Not in one single year. Newspapers led every year from 1918 to 1949, and took 36.7 percent of all US ad money in 1949 [e2-medium-001]. Radio peaked at 14.9 percent in 1945, which was less than half the newspaper share in the same year [e2-medium-002].

Direct mail is the other correction. It took 14.5 percent of US ad money in 1949 and was the second-largest named medium in most years the record splits by medium [e2-medium-004]. It slipped to third in 1944 and fourth in 1945 and 1946, when paper was short and radio and magazines passed it. Its best year was 1938, at 16.8 percent. The measured-response tradition, with its coded coupons and side-by-side tests, is therefore a story of the 1920s and 1930s. It is not a thing that starts in the 1970s.

One conflict stays open, and we leave it open. On the standard long-run series radio was 3.9 percent of all US ad money in 1932. Broadcast histories report "nearly 11 percent" for the same year, on a narrower base of national media only. We could not resolve it, so we carry both, and the band on that number runs from 3.5 to 11 [e2-medium-003].

Two large pieces of this market cannot be split apart in these years. The first is the leftover category the series calls Miscellaneous, which holds phone directories, transit ads, in-store displays and free gifts. It was 19.6 percent of 1949 spend, and directories do not get a line of their own until 1980 [e2-medium-005]. The second is classified, the want ads, which sit inside the newspaper total. We estimate want ads were about 18 percent of newspaper ad revenue in 1949, in a band from 14 to 22 percent [e2-buyers-006]. In dollars that is about $340 million, and it could be anywhere from $270 million to $420 million [e2-scale-012]. No source reports it separately before 1950, so this is a triangulated number and we say so.

TV enters the record in 1949 at $58 million, which is 1.1 percent of the market [e2-medium-006]. The next era is already on air.

## Who was paying

Four kinds of money ran through this market, and they behaved differently.

National buyers, the brand advertisers, were 57.4 percent of all US ad dollars in 1949 [e2-buyers-004]. Local buyers, mostly shops and dealers, were the other 42.6 percent [e2-buyers-005]. The series counts all direct mail as national, so strip that out and brand money in media comes to about $2,234 million, roughly 43 percent of the total [e2-scale-010]. Local retail money, once want ads are removed, comes to about $1,880 million, roughly 36 percent [e2-scale-011]. Both of those are our own splits and both are graded C.

Direct-response money went through the mail. Direct mail was $756 million in 1949 [e2-scale-013]. Per home that works out at about $17.60 of advertising mail a year, up from about $8.70 in 1935 [e2-buyers-007].

## The war made ads nearly free

In October 1942 Congress replaced a graduated tax on excess wartime profits with a flat rate of 90 percent. The Treasury had already confirmed that goodwill advertising, the kind with a war theme and no product in it, stayed a deductible cost. Put those two rules together and the arithmetic decided itself.

For a firm in that tax bracket, a dollar of advertising cost about 20 cents after tax. Two features of the law, a ceiling on the total tax and a post-war refund credit, push that figure around, so the range runs from 10 to 28 cents [e2-events-005]. The rest was money the Treasury was going to take anyway. Firms with nothing to sell kept buying ads, and told the country about their war work instead.

The data carries the fingerprint. Brand buyers were 51.7 percent of all US ad money in 1935 and 61.3 percent in 1944 and 1945 [e2-buyers-002]. The mix moved too. Between 1942 and 1945 business papers grew 108 percent and magazines 92 percent, while newspapers grew just 15 percent and direct mail fell 12 percent [e2-medium-007]. That pattern fits a paper shortage rather than weak demand, though we could not source the rationing rules well enough to claim it.

The industry also bought itself a defence. Agencies and media groups raised about $100,000 and set up the Advertising Council on 26 February 1942 [e2-events-004]. It ran war bond, salvage and recruitment work with the government. The timing was not an accident: Congress was asking at that moment whether advertising should stay deductible at all.

## Scale, and a number that spoils a modern story

US ad spend was $1,930 million in 1919 on the standard series. An older series puts the same year at $2,282 million, and we show both rather than pick [e2-scale-001]. It reached $5,210 million in 1949 [e2-scale-002]. That is growth of about 3.4 percent a year in cash terms, and the range around it runs from 2.7 to 4.1 percent [e2-scale-007].

The middle of the era is a crater. From the 1929 peak of $2,850 million the market fell 53.5 percent, to $1,325 million in 1933. On the older series the same fall is 62.0 percent [e2-scale-003]. It is still the deepest peacetime drop the record holds, and it took the rest of the decade and a war to undo.

Measured against the size of the economy, the story turns over. Ad spend hit 3.0 percent of GDP in 1922. GDP is the value of everything the country produces in a year, and 3.0 percent is the highest reading in the whole 1919 to 2007 record [e2-scale-004]. It fell to 1.2 percent in 1944, the lowest reading, because the war economy grew far faster than ad budgets did [e2-scale-005]. It stood at 1.9 percent in 1949 [e2-scale-006]. Anyone who says a later era took an unheard-of share of the economy has to get past 1922 first.

The world figure is weak and we will not dress it up. World ad spend in 1949 was on the order of $7.5 billion, with the US roughly 70 percent of it. No world survey exists for these years. We built the number two ways and both landed in the same region, but it could be $6 billion or it could be $10 billion [e2-scale-008].

One visible seam sits at the start of the long chart. The standard series puts 1938 radio at about 1.66 times the air-time sales the FCC audited for the whole industry that year [e2-scale-009]. The likely reason is that the series was built from published card rates, while the FCC counted cash after discounts and agency cuts. Some of the gap may be program spend. We flag the seam rather than smooth it.

## The line Wanamaker never said

The most quoted sentence in advertising belongs to this era, and it is a legend.

"Half the money I spend on advertising is wasted; the trouble is I don't know which half." That line first appears in print in 1890, in the trade paper Printers' Ink, with no name attached. The earliest version crediting John Wanamaker comes in 1919, and it is a third party at a bible conference doing the crediting. No written or spoken instance from Wanamaker himself has ever been found [e2-measurement-009].

The habit the quote stands for is real. The doubt about waste is real. The attribution is not, and we will not repeat it as fact.

## What the era hands on

At the end of 1949 the sponsor still owns the show, and the network still sells time. The rate card is a public document, the rating is a contract term, and the middleman still takes 15 percent for buying media and making the content.

Newspapers are still the largest medium by a wide margin. Mail is still ahead of radio. TV is 1.1 percent of the market and rising fast.

Everything that killed sponsorship is already in place. Too many stations are on air. Spot money is growing faster than network money. A rating service can now price a slot to the whole country. And a new screen is coming that will make owning a whole hour far too expensive. The next era takes the show away from the advertiser, then sells it back a minute at a time.
