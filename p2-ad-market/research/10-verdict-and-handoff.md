# The verdict and the handoff

## Two engines, not one

Google built two machines. The usual story describes one.

The first machine set the price of a search. The second machine bought the searches. Each one explains what the other cannot. Run them apart and you get two half-truths that both sound complete.

## Engine one sets the price

AdWords Select launched on 20 February 2002. It ranked ads by bid times click rate. Then it charged the winner the least it needed to hold its slot.

Google published its own worked case. Three buyers bid $1.00, $0.60 and $0.50. They pay $0.61, $0.51 and one cent. The winner pays a cent above the next bid, not its own bid.

From August 2005 each keyword also carried a floor. The floor came from its Quality Score, which Google computed. Measured relevance had become a price, not just a rank.

Here the record kills a popular claim. Quality weighting did not let Google charge more per click. It sold many more clicks at a lower price each. Slots are the scarce good, so ranking by expected revenue fills them better. Any account that says the design raised click prices has the sign backwards.

Yield did rise across the era. Google earned about half a cent of ad money per search on its own sites in 2002. By 2007 it earned about 2.2 cents, on a range of 1.6 to 2.8 cents. That range is not sloppiness. Google has never published a query count, so the divisor is an estimate and the claim is graded C.

## Engine two buys the searches

In May 2002 Google bought AOL's search traffic. The reported terms were a revenue guarantee near $100m and a revenue share near 85%. Google held $33.6m of cash at the end of 2001. The headline was bigger than the company.

Be careful with those two terms. The contract was never filed, so both trace to second-hand accounts and are graded B. What the filings do show is the result. AOL supplied about 15% of Google's total revenue in 2002.

The cash was not the costly leg. Google also issued a warrant over 7,437,452 shares. AOL's parent later took about $1.13bn out of it. That is more than eleven times the cash guarantee. Google then paid $1bn for a stake in AOL in 2006 and lost about $717m on it.

The partner engine bought position, not profit. Google Network sites peaked at 48.7% of total revenue in 2004. That figure is gross, and the gross basis flatters the engine. Net of what Google paid its partners, the network gave only 9.4% of 2008 ad revenue.

The take rate tells the same story. Google kept about 9% of network ad money in 2002, rose to 24.7% by 2006, then fell back to 21.3% in 2008. A take rate is an outcome of competition for partners. It is not a knob the seller sets.

The two dollars were never the same business. In 2008 Google kept 95.5 cents of every ad dollar earned on its own pages, against 21.3 cents on partner pages.

## Why the two are one sentence

The most anyone can pay for a default is what a query earns, less what it costs to serve. So the firm with the higher yield wins the deal by construction. The auction set the size of the cheque.

The payouts leave a signature. Google paid out 91% of network ad revenue in 2002. Overture paid out 58% of its revenue that year. That gap of more than thirty points is the auction paying for the deal.

This also kills the neat version of the Overture story. Overture was not a failing product. It booked $667.7m in 2002 against Google's $439.5m of total revenue. Its average realised price per click was $0.31. Yahoo did not copy quality weighting until 5 February 2007, five years late.

So the better auction cannot be the whole story. Overture lost on traffic. About 60% of its 2002 revenue came through Microsoft and Yahoo. One of them became its owner and the other became its rival.

## How much credit each engine gets

The verdict is bounded, and it goes against both simple stories.

- On gross growth, partner sites supplied 31.9% of the rise in Google's ad revenue from 2002 to 2008. Google's own sites supplied 68.1%.
- Net of partner payments, the network was 9.4% of 2008 ad revenue. Total ad revenue that year was $21.1bn.
- Inside owned-site growth, 52% to 56% came from more queries. The rest, 44% to 48%, came from more money per query. That is a band, not a point, and it is graded C.
- Most of that query growth was not bought. Money paid to send searches to Google's own site was 3.7% of owned-site ad revenue in 2007.

Read together: the deals bought the beachhead, and the auction paid for the deals and converted the traffic. The pure-deals story only becomes right after the era, once defaults take most of the traffic budget.

## What Google did not change

**The seller still counts the audience.** No industry standard for a valid click existed until 2009, a year after the auction era closed. Google gave web analytics away free from November 2005, so the seller also supplied the buyer's ruler. It reports how fast paid clicks grow. It has never published a click count, an average click price, or a query count.

The habit outlived the era. Facebook paid $40m in 2019 to settle a case over video counts it had overstated. Nielsen lost its accreditation in 2021 after pandemic undercounts, which one trade group priced at $468m or more of lost network ad revenue. By 2025 more than half of global web traffic was bots.

Here the record kills a favourite line. "Half my advertising is wasted" is usually put in John Wanamaker's mouth. It first appears in print in 1890 with no name on it. The earliest link to Wanamaker is from 1919, made by someone else at a conference. No instance from Wanamaker himself has ever been found. The saying is legend. The problem it names is real and is still here.

**The middleman did not die.** Omnicom closed its purchase of Interpublic in November 2025, forming a holding company with over $25bn of revenue. In 2023, 82% of surveyed member marketers also ran an in-house agency. The agent multiplied. It did not vanish.

**Ads did not take a bigger share of the economy.** US ad spend hit 3.0% of GDP in 1922, the highest reading in the long series. It was 2.0% in 2007 and about 1.32% in 2025. The high point after 1960 is 2000, at about 2.5%, and the series never gets back to 1922. So the folklore that ads hold a steady 2% of GDP is built by quoting the recent window and dropping the 1920s.

**The money pools moved far less than the media did.** Local money was 48.3% of the US total in 1935 and 33.7% in 2007. Direct mail was the largest single line in the long US series through 2007, at $60.2bn.

This is why the era names in this project name a mechanism, not a medium. A medium can be famous and small. Radio never led US ad spend. Its share peaked at 14.9% in 1945, under half the newspaper share that year. It still held only 6.7% in 1993.

**The sell side was already tight.** The top ten sellers took 72% of US internet ad revenue in the last quarter of 2008. By 2025 the top ten took 84.1%. Google made the market tighter. It did not make it concentrated.

## The auction died, the lever did not

Header bidding broke the display waterfall in the mid-2010s. Publishers began running one parallel auction in the page header, so every exchange bid at once. Google's open-web display exchange then moved to a single first-price auction on 5 September 2019. The stated reason was transparency. There is no yield trick inside a first-price rule, because rational buyers shade their bids and the seller collects about the same.

Search went the other way in the same year. Google's rGSP auction raised top-slot click prices by 5.91% on PCs and tablets in tests. A 5.74% revenue gain still held two months after launch. Buyers cannot opt out.

Do not merge the two 2019 changes. Display went first-price. Search never did. This is the most common factual error in accounts of that year, and both halves of our record flag it.

The court record names three search pricing knobs: squashing, format pricing and rGSP. Format pricing had grown to about 20% of Google's search text-ad revenue per thousand queries. Google's ads chief testified that price "tunings" moved prices by about 5% on average, and allowed that 10% was possible on some queries. Buyers were not told.

Our two engines read this differently, and we keep both readings. The auction side says the lever is old. From August 2005 a keyword's floor was its price whenever only one buyer showed up, so moving that floor was already a pure price move. The deals side says the power is new. By 2019 the court credited the gains to monopoly rather than to design. Both hold. The lever is old and the power behind it is new. Neither side corrects the other.

## The deals outlived the auction

In 2008, 11.0% of Google's traffic payments bought traffic for its own sites. By 2021, 57.7% did. That year Google paid $26.3bn to be the search default. The 2022 payment to Apple alone was about $20bn, at a rate of 36% of the search ad revenue earned through Safari.

The court found the challenged contracts covered about half of all US general search queries. Google's own 2017 estimate put defaults behind 54% of its search revenue. Its internal work projected that losing the Safari default would cost 60% to 80% of iOS queries. Google held 89.2% of US general search queries in 2020. It had tested 5% text-ad price rises again and again, and found that it could hold them.

The ruling landed on 5 August 2024. The remedy landed on 2 September 2025. It barred exclusive deals and capped terms at one year. It did not stop the payments and did not order a Chrome sale. A reader who treats the 2024 ruling as the end of the payments has it wrong. Google paid $59.9bn of traffic payments in 2025, or 20.3% of ad revenue.

## The trio: money in, cost, margin

| Era and year | Unit | Money per unit | Cost per unit | Margin | Grades |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Era 5, 2000 | one web ad impression | $0.007 | $0.0009 | 85%, gross | C, C, C |
| Era 6, 2007 | one Google search query | $0.022 | $0.0035 | 85%, gross | C, C, C |
| Era 7, 2025 | one Google search query | $0.037 | $0.0065 | 40.7%, operating | C, C, A |

Read that table with care. Every figure in the first two rows is our best estimate, graded C. The last row is mixed. Money per query could be anywhere from 3 to 5 cents. Cost per query could be anywhere from 0.4 to 1.0 cents. Only the 40.7% is hard, and it comes straight from the 10-K.

The margins are also not the same ruler. Eras 5 and 6 give gross margin on one unit: what it earns, less what it costs to serve. Era 7 gives an operating margin for a whole segment, after staff, sales and $59.9bn of traffic payments. So 85% falling to 40.7% is not a collapse in unit margin. It is a change of measure, and P3 must not read it as a trend.

One comparison does travel cleanly. In 2020 Google spent about 0.65 cents running search and search ads for one query. In 2021 it paid about 0.8 cents per query for defaults. It paid more to be found than it spent answering.

Era 5 carries the sharpest warning. A web ad impression in 2000 earned about $0.007 and cost about $0.0009 to serve. Gross margin was near 85%. The sellers still lost money, because selling an impression cost far more than serving one. A fat unit margin is not a business.

## The cost series P3 inherits

One standard answer, held to a fixed length, priced at the cheapest capable model on sale:

- 2023: about 4.8 cents.
- 2024: about 0.033 cents, a fall of roughly 145 times in twelve months.
- 2025: about 0.022 cents.
- Mid-2026: still about 0.022 cents.

Now set that against what a search earns. In 2023 one machine answer cost roughly 130% of the ad revenue of one query. By mid-2026 the floor cost was roughly 0.6% of it.

Calibration matters more than the trend here. All four points are graded C and the bands are wide. The 2024 point runs from 0.025 cents up to 0.95 cents. That spread is not noise about one model's price. It is the gap between the cheap tier and the frontier tier, and which tier answers dominates the whole figure.

The floor has also stopped falling. The cheap tier in mid-2026 costs what it cost in 2025. Meanwhile frontier reasoning models push the cost per answer back up, because they emit far more tokens than a fixed-length answer allows.

So the blunt cost objection to ad-funded AI is much weaker than it was in 2023. That is a fact about the floor price of a cheap answer. It is not a verdict about the business.

## Where things stood on 30 June 2026

US ad spend was about $405bn in 2025, or about 1.32% of GDP. About 74% of it was digital, on a grade-C range of 72% to 82%. Search alone was $114.2bn of US internet ad revenue. About 90% of non-search digital revenue was traded by machine.

Google's global ad revenue was $294.7bn in 2025. Meta's was $196.2bn and Amazon's ad services were $68.6bn. The three together took about 71.9% of US digital ad spending.

The old intent media are gone as pools, not as demand. US directory ad revenue fell from about $14.7bn in 2005 to about $1.1bn in 2024. National direct-response money was about $130bn in 2025, on a grade C estimate.

Machine answers reached the results page. AI Overviews passed 2 billion monthly users by July 2025, and Google says the format earns about as much per query as classic search. Pew's browsing panel found users clicked a result on 8% of visits with a summary, against 15% of visits without one. Both findings sit in the record. They are not yet reconciled.

Three things were open at the freeze and carry no claim, on purpose. The ad-tech remedies opinion had not issued. Appeals of both Google rulings were expected and unresolved. OpenAI's ad pilot in ChatGPT was live in the US only, with stated plans to widen. We mark them as pointers, because they moved after the freeze and a frozen record cannot hold them.

## What P3 inherits as open

1. **Can an answer carry an auction?** Google's design needs many bidders per query and a rank of slots to sort them into. A generated answer offers far fewer places to stand.
2. **Does intent survive the answer?** The click gap is 8% against 15%, while the seller reports flat earnings per query. Both cannot be the whole picture.
3. **The coupling nobody measured.** A thin take rate on partner sites is usually defended this way: more inventory pulls in more buyers, deepens the auction and lifts prices on the seller's own pages. Google never published the buyer counts, auction depth or owned-site click prices needed to test that. We record it as unmeasured, never as a finding. P3 should care, because a lab defending a cheap or free tier will make the same argument.
4. **The knobs do not add up.** Format pricing was about 20% of search text-ad revenue per thousand queries, yet rGSP replaced it for a lasting gain of 5.74%. Either the 20% was not all extra, or the two knobs taxed the same surplus. We leave it unresolved.
5. **Do not borrow era 7's evidence for era 6.** Once defaults foreclosed about half the market, later price gains cannot be credited to auction design. The court credited them to power.
6. **The divisor problem.** Money per query and cost per query are graded C in both eras for the same reason. Google has never published a query count. Any per-query number P3 builds on inherits that band.
7. **The inversion P3 must price.** Search had a near-zero marginal cost and a fat gross margin, and it funded everything. AI has a real marginal cost per answer, even at the floor. Era 5 is the standing warning: high gross margin on the unit, heavy losses at the company.
