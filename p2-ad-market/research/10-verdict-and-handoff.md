# The verdict and the handoff

The last chapter answered where the money went. The money is settled. The counting is not.

One question is left, and it runs under all seven eras. Who counted the audience, and who paid them to count it?

Every price in this history rests on somebody's count. An advertiser buys attention it cannot see, so somebody has to say how much attention was there. From 1914 to 1996, eighty-two years, that somebody was never the seller.

**Then it was.** The seller taking over the count is the one change nobody had to legislate, buy or announce.

Here is the handover, in order. Each entry names who held the tape measure, who paid them, and what it did to the price.

## Who held the tape measure

**1914. The Audit Bureau of Circulations.** A publisher-run audit body and an advertiser-run one merged in Chicago and became the world's first media audit. Advertisers, agencies and publishers all paid for it together, which is why no one side could bend it. It counted copies printed and sold, twice a year on the publisher's word and once a year on its own. Before it, publishers stated their own circulation and buyers had no way to test the figure, so a newspaper's printed asking price meant very little. After it, the asking price meant something a buyer could check.

**1930. The Cooperative Analysis of Broadcasting.** Radio had no copies to count, so the buyers built a counter of their own. Interviewers rang homes and asked what the family had heard the day before. Forty-nine advertisers paid $70 a month and the first year brought in $33,045. The buyers kept the networks selling the airtime off the subscriber list until 1937. Buyers were counting the sellers' goods, and an hour of radio became a thing you could put a price on.

The method had a hole in it from the start. Only 31 percent of American families had a telephone in the mid-1930s, while 96 percent of telephone owners had a radio. Every rating of the era therefore leaned towards the well-off.

**1934. Clark-Hooper.** A rival medium bought its own count. Magazine publishers, who believed the buyers' service was flattering radio, paid Clark-Hooper to ring homes during the show itself, in sixteen cities. The first ratings came in lower than the buyers' service, which is exactly what the publishers had paid to see. No episode in this history shows more plainly that the funder of a count shapes the count.

The sting came ten years later, from the other side. By 1944 both services were asking the same question the same way, and Hooper's answers still ran about 20 percent above the buyers' service. Same method, different numbers. Buyer-run counting did not survive the finding, and from 1946 the counters sold to both sides at once.

**1950. Nielsen.** In March 1950 Nielsen bought Hooper's national radio and television business. One private firm then supplied the number behind every national broadcast price for twenty-five years, and it sold that number to the networks selling the time and to the agencies buying it. The panel was tiny against the money it moved. About 1,200 metered homes stood behind every network price, roughly one metered home for every 54,000 American television homes in 1973. The counter now answered to the counted, and both sides paid it.

**1964. The Broadcast Rating Council.** Congress held hearings on how the ratings firms made their numbers, running from March 1963 to September 1964. Rather than regulate, the committee pushed the trade to audit itself, and the industry funded the Broadcast Rating Council, later the Media Rating Council. It set no prices. It decided whether the number that set them could be trusted. A private auditor the industry pays for is a weak thing, but a buyer who doubted a rating had somewhere to take the doubt. The web arrived with nothing of the kind.

**1987. The people meter.** In September 1987 Nielsen swapped paper diaries and set-top counters for a box each viewer pressed a button on. Measured prime-time viewing fell about 10 percent overnight, and the networks owed free replacement airtime scarcer than the time they had already sold. Chapter 5 records what the change cost each of them. The count moved the money without a single viewer moving.

**1994. The ad-server log.** The first banner ads went live on the website HotWired on 27 October 1994, twelve brands at launch. No auditor came with them. The counting machine was the ad server, the software that picks and delivers each ad, and the seller owned it. Its log was the invoice.

In 1996 a trade body put the first number on US web ad money: $267 million. That number came off the sellers' own machines. Eighty-two years after the Audit Bureau, the count had moved inside the firm collecting the cheque.

Buyers saw it and could do nothing. Procter & Gamble called the industry to Cincinnati in August 1998 to argue about it, and only 16 percent of marketers said they were satisfied with online ad measurement. Rival counters existed and disagreed with each other. Server logs missed pages a reader's browser had already stored. Panels of watched users missed everyone online at work and at school. Neither method could beat the other, so neither became the currency. By 2000 DoubleClick's server alone delivered 621 billion ads worldwide, and Yahoo reported its own page views with nobody checking.

**2002. The seller's own predicted click.** On 20 February 2002 Google ranked search ads by the bid times how often it expected each ad to be clicked. Google made that forecast itself. It decided which clicks were real. It billed against its own log. From August 2005 the same in-house score set each keyword's lowest allowed bid, so on a search with only one bidder Google set the price outright.

No industry standard for a valid click existed until 2009, a year after the era closed. In November 2005 Google gave its measuring tool away free, and 100,000 accounts opened in the first week, so buyers now checked the seller's work inside the seller's own instrument. Google began reporting how fast its paid clicks were growing. It has never published a click count, an average click price or a query count.

**2019. An undisclosed exponent.** In 2024 a US court set out three ways Google had moved the price of a search ad. Google's own staff called them intentional pricing. One of them, nicknamed Butternut Squash inside the company, takes the predicted click rate and raises it to a power below one. That shrinks the gap between an ad people click and an ad they ignore, and the winner's price climbs towards the runner-up's raw bid. Google has never published the number in that power, so no outside party can work out what it did. Google did not tell the buyers either.

## What the handover cost

Counting did not get worse after 1994. It got far more precise and far cheaper. The buyer simply lost the right to argue with it.

For eighty-two years a buyer who doubted the number could go to somebody else: an auditor, a rival service, a council the trade paid for. After 1994 the buyer could only ask the seller.

The failures since are the ones that arrangement predicts, and chapter 8 sets out three of them. Facebook overstated how long people watched its videos and paid $40 million to settle the advertisers' case. The Media Rating Council pulled Nielsen's seal of approval in 2021 over pandemic undercounts that one trade body priced at $468 million or more. And the thing being counted grew harder to count, with bots now more than half of global web traffic.

One question the ledger does not settle is which of Google's two engines did the work: the auction that set the price of a search, or the deals that bought the searches. The auction chapter weighs them and finds them close to even, with partner sites supplying 31.9 percent of the era's revenue growth and Google's own sites 68.1 percent.

## What Google did not change

**The middleman did not die.** Omnicom closed its purchase of Interpublic in November 2025, forming a holding company with over $25bn of revenue. In 2023, 82% of surveyed member marketers also ran an in-house agency. The agent multiplied. It did not vanish.

**Ads did not take a bigger share of the economy.** US ad spend hit 3.0% of GDP in 1922, the highest reading in the long series. It was 2.0% in 2007 and about 1.32% in 2025. The high point after 1960 is 2000, at about 2.5%, and the series never gets back to 1922. People build the steady-2% folklore by quoting the recent window and dropping the 1920s.

**The money pools moved far less than the media did.** Local money was 48.3% of the US total in 1935 and 33.7% in 2007. Direct mail was the largest single line in the long US series through 2007, at $60.2bn.

That stability is why the era names in this project name a mechanism rather than a medium. A medium can be famous and small: radio never led US ad spend, peaking at 14.9% in 1945 and holding 6.7% in 1993.

**The sell side was already tight.** The top ten sellers took 72% of US internet ad revenue in the last quarter of 2008. By 2025 the top ten took 84.1%. Google made the market tighter. It did not make it concentrated.

## The trio: money in, cost, margin

| Year and era | Unit | Money per unit | Cost per unit | Margin | Grades |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2000, the impression years | one web display impression | $0.007 | $0.0009 | 85%, gross | C, C, C |
| 2007, the auction years | one Google search query | $0.022 | $0.0035 | 85%, gross | C, C, C |
| 2025, the machine market | one Google search query | $0.037 | $0.0065 | 40.7%, operating | C, C, A |

Read that table with care. Every figure in the first two rows is our best estimate, graded C. The last row is mixed. Money per query could be anywhere from 3 to 5 cents. Cost per query could be anywhere from 0.4 to 1.0 cents. Only the 40.7% is hard, and it comes straight from Google's audited annual report to US regulators.

The margins are also not the same ruler. The first two rows give gross margin on one unit: what it earns, less what it costs to serve. The last row gives an operating margin for a whole business segment, after staff, sales and $59.9bn paid out to other firms for sending traffic. The fall from 85% to 40.7% is not a collapse in unit margin. It is a change of measure, and the next project must not read it as a trend.

One comparison does travel cleanly. In 2020 Google spent about 0.65 cents running search and search ads for one query. In 2021 it paid about 0.8 cents per query to be the search engine already switched on when a phone or browser leaves the factory. It paid more to be found than it spent answering.

The dot-com years carry the sharpest warning. A web display impression in 2000 earned about $0.007 of banner and sponsorship money and cost about $0.0009 to serve. Gross margin was near 85%. The sellers still lost money, because selling an impression cost far more than serving one. A fat unit margin is not a business.

## The cost series the next project inherits

One standard answer, held to a fixed length, priced at the cheapest capable model on sale:

- 2023: about 4.8 cents.
- 2024: 0.033 cents, a fall of exactly 145.45 times in twelve months.
- 2025: about 0.0165 cents.
- Mid-2026: about 0.022 cents.

Now set that against what a search earns. In 2023 one machine answer cost roughly 130% of the ad revenue of one query. By mid-2026 the floor cost was roughly 0.6% of it.

Read this as two lines, not one line with a wide band. Every point above is the cheapest capable model on a public price list, and each is arithmetic on a printed price rather than an estimate. That is why the 2023 to 2024 drop is exact: 0.048 divided by 0.00033. The top tier is the second line, and it runs far higher. It cost 0.95 cents an answer in 2024, 0.43 cents in 2025, and 0.7 to 1.4 cents at the freeze.

The floor stopped falling, then ticked back up. Google shut down the model that set the 2025 floor on 1 June 2026, and every cheap model launched since costs more than it did. The newest and most expensive AI models push the cost of an answer higher still, because they write far more words per answer than a fixed length allows, and the AI firms charge by the amount of text.

The blunt cost objection to ad-funded AI is much weaker than it was in 2023. That is a fact about the floor price of a cheap answer. It is not a verdict about the business.

## Where things stood on 30 June 2026

US ad spend was about $405bn in 2025, or about 1.32% of GDP. About 74% of it was digital, on a grade-C range of 72% to 82%. Search alone was $114.2bn of US internet ad revenue. Machines traded about 90% of the non-search digital revenue.

Google's global ad revenue was $294.7bn in 2025. Meta's was $196.2bn and Amazon's ad services were $68.6bn. The three together took about 71.9% of US digital ad spending on EMARKETER's definition of digital, or 53.2% of the whole market on the MAGNA and IAB rail behind that 74%.

The old intent media are gone as pools, not as demand. US directory ad revenue fell from about $14.7bn in 2005 to about $1.1bn in 2024. National direct-response money was about $151.2bn in 2025, on a grade C estimate.

Machine answers reached the results page. AI Overviews passed 2 billion monthly users by July 2025, and Google says the format earns about as much per query as classic search. Pew's browsing panel found users clicked a result on 8% of visits with a summary, against 15% of visits without one. Both findings sit in the record, and nobody has yet squared them.

Three things were open at the freeze and carry no claim, on purpose. In the second Google case, the one about the software behind display ads, the judge had not yet said what Google must do to put things right. Appeals of both Google rulings were expected and unresolved. OpenAI's ad pilot in ChatGPT was live in the US only, with stated plans to widen. We mark them as pointers, because they moved after the freeze and a frozen record cannot hold them.

## What the next project inherits as open

1. **Can an answer carry an auction?** Google's design needs many bidders per query and a rank of slots to sort them into. A generated answer offers far fewer places to stand.
2. **Does intent survive the answer?** The click gap is 8% against 15%, while the seller reports flat earnings per query. Both cannot be the whole picture.
3. **The coupling nobody measured.** Sellers usually defend a thin cut on partner sites this way: more space pulls in more buyers, deepens the auction and lifts prices on their own pages. Google's cut on partner money ran from about 9% in 2002 to 21.3% in 2008, and it never published the buyer counts, auction depth or own-site click prices needed to test the defence. We record it as unmeasured, never as a finding. The next project should care, because a lab defending a cheap or free tier will make the same argument.
4. **The knobs do not add up.** Format pricing was about 20% of search text-ad revenue per thousand queries, yet the 2019 auction change replaced it for a lasting gain of 5.74%. Either the 20% was not all extra, or the two knobs taxed the same surplus. We leave it unresolved.
5. **Do not borrow the machine market's evidence for the auction years.** Once default deals covered about half the US search market, later price rises cannot be credited to auction design. The court credited them to power, and it found that Google had tested 5% text-ad price rises again and again and made them stick.
6. **The divisor problem.** Money per query and cost per query are graded C in both eras for the same reason. Google has never published a query count. Any per-query number the next project builds on inherits that band.
7. **The inversion the next project must price.** Search had a near-zero cost per extra query and a fat gross margin, and it funded everything. AI has a real cost per answer, even at the floor. The dot-com years are the standing warning: high gross margin on the unit, heavy losses at the company.

Change the counting and you change the price. For eighty-two years the counter was somebody other than the seller. It no longer is, and every number the next project touches was produced by the firm being paid.
