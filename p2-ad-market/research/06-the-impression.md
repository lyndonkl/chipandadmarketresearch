# The Impression

*Era 5 — 1994 to 2001. The web arrives and sells attention by the thousand.*

The last chapter left the market waiting for a machine that could sell a stated want by auction. That machine took seven years to arrive, and it sold something else first.

In October 1994 a web magazine called HotWired switched on paid ads. Twelve brands went live at once, though some accounts say fourteen.[^1] No one banner came first. A reader could tap one of those ads and be carried to the advertiser's own page. That tap is a click, and the share of people shown an ad who make it is the click-through rate. The rate people still quote from that launch was self-reported, and the people who were there no longer agree on it. The banner is not the point anyway. The price tag is.

HotWired sold launch sponsorships. It set the price by looking at what a page cost in the print magazine. That one move shaped the next seven years.

The web could have sold almost anything. It could have sold a click, a lead, a sale, or an hour of a person's time. It sold the impression: one ad, shown once, to one pair of eyes. And it sold impressions the way magazines sold pages, by the thousand. The trade calls that price a CPM — the cost per thousand impressions.

## A unit has to be standard before anyone can trade it

A buyer cannot compare two sites until the thing being bought is the same shape on both. Print solved this in 1914 with audited circulation. The web solved it on 10 December 1996, when the Internet Advertising Bureau and a second trade body published eight voluntary banner sizes.[^2] The bureau, the IAB from here on, is a trade body of the sellers: its members are the sites and networks that sell the space. The sellers set the shape of the unit they sold. The IAB also counts how much web ad money changes hands each year, and this chapter and the ones after it lean on that count. The wide strip called the full banner became the shape every ad had to fit.

Agreeing those sizes was a small act with large effects. Once the unit is standard, the impression becomes a commodity. Once it is a commodity, it has a spot price. Almost everything else in this era follows from that.

## The web was tiny. The market was not.

Total US measured ad spend ran from $153.0B in 1994 to $247.5B in 2000.[^3] That is the biggest run in the whole post-war record.

Web ad money inside it was small and hard to see. Nobody audited 1995 at all. We estimate about $45M, and we would not argue with anything from $25M to $80M.[^4] The first measured year was 1996, at $267M.[^5] By 2000 the web took about $8.1B.[^6] Even that figure is contested: the Coen series carries $6.5B for the same year, because it counts what advertisers billed rather than what sellers booked.

How big was the web as a share of the market at its peak? We estimate 3.3%, and the honest range is 2.6% to 3.7%.[^7] The spread is not sloppiness. It is what happens when the top and bottom of a fraction come from different surveys.

Meanwhile the old media did not fall over. Newspapers were the largest single US medium every year from 1994 through 2000, ending at $49.05B.[^8] The medium that finally took the top spot in 2001 was not the web. It was direct mail, at $44.7B.[^9] Yellow Pages grew right through the web's arrival, from $9.8B in 1994 to $13.2B in 2000.[^10] Newspaper classified peaked in 2000 at $19.6B, about 40% of all newspaper ad revenue, with job ads alone at $8.7B.[^11] That peak came six years after the web arrived.

Worldwide, the era's high point was roughly $335B in 2000 on a major-media basis. No free audited global series exists, so treat that as a bracket of $300B to $380B rather than a count.[^12]

One warning for anyone drawing this era on a chart. In 2000 the Coen series swapped its "Billboards" category for a wider "Out of Home" one worth 2.77 times as much.[^13] Splice the two lines without a note and you invent a boom that never happened.

## Where the money actually sat

Sort the 2000 market by the job the money was doing, not by the medium carrying it. Four pools appear:

| Money type | 2000 spend | Share | 80% range |
|---|---|---|---|
| National brand | $94.9B | 38.3% | 31.5-43.0% |
| Local retail | $70.1B | 28.3% | 20.8-33.0% |
| Direct response | $61.2B | 24.7% | 19.0-36.0% |
| Classified | $21.9B | 8.9% | 7.9-10.1% |

This split is ours, not a published series.[^14] We ran three methods at it and took the middle answer, so it no longer sums to exactly 100%. It runs 0.2 points over, and we show that rather than shave it. Grade it C, except the newspaper classified inside the fourth row, which is a real published series and grades B.

You cannot rank two of these rows against each other. Local retail and direct response sit 3.6 points apart, their ranges overlap almost completely, and the order flips on one bookkeeping choice: where the $13.2B of Yellow Pages money goes.[^61] The table above uses this era's own rule, which splits that line by who bought it and leaves the $11.1B local part in local money, so local retail leads at 28.3% against 24.7%. The other rule sends the whole line to the response pool, which is what the later chapters and our own data layer do. On it the order reverses: direct response is $72.3B, or 29.2%, and local retail is $58.9B, or 23.8%. That one block is the entire difference. No source of the time asked a Yellow Pages buyer what it thought it was buying, so nothing can settle which rule is right, and we carry both. Use the comparable rule whenever you set 2000 against another year, never sort those two bars on a chart, and note that national brand and classified do not move at all.

Now set the web against it. The whole internet ad market in 2000 was about $8.1B. Classified alone was more than twice that. Direct mail alone was more than five times it. Almost none of the local or classified money had moved: under 3% of the classified pool was online at the top of the boom.[^15]

Those figures kill a popular story. Measurable, response-priced advertising did not arrive with the web. On the comparable rule, 29.2% of all US ad money was already paying for a counted reply, and it ran on mail and directories.

## Who sold it

Web ad space concentrated faster than any earlier medium. The ten largest sellers took 71% of US internet ad revenue in the last quarter of 2000, and 77% a year later. The top 25 took 91%, the top 50 took 98%.[^16] Search engines and portals alone were 36% of revenue in 2000.[^17]

The biggest of them ran a magazine business with a server attached. Yahoo booked $1,110.2M of net revenue in 2000, of which $968.3M was banners and sponsorships.[^18]

Between the portal and the advertiser sat a middle layer that took a real cut. DoubleClick took $505.6M in 2000, doing three jobs. It sold space on other people's sites and paid them a share of what it collected. It ran the software that picked and delivered each ad. And it sold data.[^19] Its network arm kept 25.3 cents of every dollar it sold in 2000, or 32.6 cents once we strip out a one-off write-off. That share is the gross margin. Because its costs were mostly payments to publishers, the margin is also the take rate. It swung hard from year to year: DoubleClick kept 21.2 cents in 1998 and 39.8 cents in 1999. Read across those three years and publishers kept roughly 60% to 79% of network-sold money.[^20]

At the top of the market the deal looked older than that. Portals sold "anchor tenancy" — a fixed fee for guaranteed placement, priced like a cover position in a magazine. Excite, a search site, paid the browser maker Netscape about $5.0M a year to sit on one of Netscape's pages. The company reports we can read bundle several payments together, so read that as $4.0M to $8.25M.[^21] The radio sponsors of the 1930s would have recognised the contract on sight.

## Who bought it

The buyer base was startlingly thin. Yahoo, the biggest seller on the web, had about 3,800 advertising customers in 1998.[^22]

Worse, much of the demand was the industry buying from itself. DoubleClick told investors that traditional advertisers had "grown to over 55%" of its network revenue by the last quarter of 2000.[^23] Read that backwards: until then, internet companies had been the majority of demand, spending money raised from investors rather than earned from customers.

You could watch that money leave the web. At Super Bowl XXXIV in January 2000, fourteen dot-com companies ran in-game spots at roughly $2.2M for thirty seconds.[^24] Three weeks earlier, AOL had agreed to buy Time Warner in a deal announced at about $165B.[^25] The web's ad boom was funding television and film, not the other way round.

Real brand money did arrive, but slowly. Consumer brands were 30% of US internet ad revenue in 2001, ahead of computing at 18%.[^26]

## Who counted it

This era is the first in which the seller owned the meter and the buyer had nowhere to appeal.

The counting technology was the ad server, and its log was the invoice. DoubleClick's DART delivered 621 billion ads worldwide in 2000.[^27] Yahoo reported an average of 900 million page views a day in December 2000, up from 470 million a year before, with no outside audit.[^28] A rival method, the user panel, produced different answers: AdRelevance measured 172 billion US impressions in the last quarter of 2000.[^29] Server logs missed cached pages. Panels missed people at work and school. Neither side could settle it.

Buyers knew. When Procter & Gamble convened the industry in Cincinnati in August 1998, only 16% of marketers said they were satisfied with online ad measurement.[^30] Two thirds said poor information was stopping them from buying.

Two numbers then did the real damage. The first was the click-through rate, which fell to about 0.3% by mid-2000 — plausibly 0.25% to 0.55%, depending on who counted.[^31] That was the metric that had justified the web's premium prices. The second was barter. Cash fell from 93% of reported internet ad revenue in 2000 to 89% in 2001, with trade deals reaching 13% in the final quarter.[^32] Sellers booked barter at list prices no cash buyer would pay, so the market's reported revenue was partly a fiction.

## The first unit economics

Here the era gets specific, and the specifics are unflattering.

The list price for a full banner was about $25 per thousand impressions at the end of 2000. That figure reaches us third-hand, so treat it as $15 to $35.[^33]

Sellers collected nothing like it. We estimate about $7 per thousand delivered display impressions in 2000, with a range of $4 to $11.[^34] That $7 counts only money paid by the impression: banner and sponsorship revenue, 76% of the IAB total. Search, classified, email and referral money is left out, because none of it was sold that way. The gap is space that never sold, discounts for buying in bulk, the site's own ads filling empty slots, and space swapped rather than sold for cash. DoubleClick said as much in its own filing: unsold inventory "diluted the effective price of delivered advertising impressions."

Serving an impression was nearly free. Content, bandwidth, equipment and ad delivery came to roughly $0.90 per thousand at portal scale, and the honest range is $0.40 to $2.50.[^35] Gross margin therefore looked wonderful: about 85% market-wide, with Yahoo's audited figure at 86.5%.[^36]

Now hold those two facts together, because their contradiction is the whole era. Gross margins beat print. Operating results were catastrophic. Yahoo spent 38% of its 2000 revenue on sales and marketing and still lost $92.8M in 2001. Networks earned only 25% to 40%, because they paid the publisher out of the same impression.

Selling an impression cost far more than serving one. That is what a hand-sold, negotiated, human-priced market costs. Nothing in this era fixed it.

## Two ways to aim

Targeting ran on two rival logics, and the more advanced one lost.

The first was the profile. A cookie is a small tag a website leaves inside your browser so it can recognise the same browser when it comes back. Cookies let DoubleClick target by site, place, browser, time of day and how often a person had already seen an ad. In 1999 it bought Abacus, whose cooperative database held purchase histories on about 90% of US households.[^37] Joining the two would have given the web named-person targeting. It never happened. A complaint to the Federal Trade Commission in February 2000 triggered an investigation, and DoubleClick abandoned the plan on 2 March 2000. The first great privacy retreat happened before behavioural targeting had really shipped.

Reach was what made profiles worth anything, and reach claims were mostly self-graded. DoubleClick said its network covered 48% of the US internet audience. That is a company's own marketing figure relayed by a reference work, so read it as 40% to 55% and treat it as a claim rather than a measurement.[^38]

The second logic was intent: match the ad to the words the person just typed. It needs no profile and no cookie. In 2000 keyword search was 1% of US internet ad revenue.[^39] It grew to 4% for 2001 and 6% in the final quarter, while everything around it shrank.

## GoTo prices the click

In June 1998 GoTo.com started selling something the rest of the market did not sell: not an impression, but a click.

The mechanism was simple enough to explain in one breath. An advertiser names a price it will pay for one click on one keyword. Listings are ranked by that price, highest first. The winner pays exactly what it bid. The floor was five cents, and a human editor reviewed every listing before it ran.[^40]

Three things follow from that design, and each one matters later.

The advertiser sets the price, not the seller. GoTo had no price list, no haggling, no salesperson. And the seller only gets paid when the ad works, so the risk of a wasted impression moves from the buyer to the seller.

It grew through the crash. GoTo made $822,000 in 1998, then $103.1M in 2000 and $288.1M in 2001.[^41] By December 2001, renamed Overture, it had about 53,000 paying advertisers, most of them small merchants who had never bought media through an agency.[^42] Entry cost a deposit of $25 to $50 and $20 a month. In the final quarter of 2001 it delivered 442 million clicks at an average realised price of $0.23.[^43]

Overture also said out loud whose money it was after. Its own filing benchmarks the business against Yellow Pages calls and direct-mail responses, not against television. It estimated that search could produce about 65 million commercial leads a day.[^44] Search was aiming at the directory and mail pools from the start.

One more number from that filing tells you where the power sat. Overture paid 56% of its 2001 revenue to the portals and browsers that sent it queries, down from 64% the year before. Running the search itself cost 7.3%.[^45] More than 95% of its traffic came from partners.[^46] The expensive part of a click was never the technology. It was distribution. The next era turns on that fact.

## The crash

US internet ad revenue fell about 12% in 2001, to roughly $7.1B — the medium's first ever down year, after four straight quarterly declines from a peak of $2.162B.[^47] Total US ad spend fell 6.5% to $231.3B, only the fifth annual fall in the series since 1934.[^48]

The deeper number is the share of the economy. US ad spend reached about 2.4% of GDP in 2000 and never got back there.[^49] Estimates run 2.3% to 2.5%. That single fact constrains every story told about the next twenty years: what follows is money moving between pools, not a bigger pie.

The wreckage was not evenly spread. Banners fell from 48% of internet ad revenue to 36%.[^50] Pure impression pricing held its ground at 43% in 2000 and 48% in 2001, while performance pricing rose from 10% to 12%.[^51] Impression selling came out of the crash smaller. Performance selling came out of it larger.

Web agencies died fastest. marchFIRST, built from two large interactive shops, lost $437M in a single quarter in 2000 and filed for bankruptcy in April 2001.[^52] That October, Excite@Home, one of the era's flagship portals, filed for Chapter 11 as well.

The traditional agency's pay model was already going, and the web did not cause it. In 1994, 61% of large US national advertisers still paid their agencies a media commission; by 2003 it was 10%.[^53] Census data show commissions and markups sliding from 70% of agency income in 1992 to 59% in 1997.[^54] The rate itself had eroded too: the famous 15% commission was, in practice, about 11% by 1997, down from 14% in 1977.[^55]

## What this era hands on

On 23 October 2000 Google opened AdWords to self-serve signups, with about 350 businesses aboard.[^56] It sold text ads beside the search results at fixed rates of $15, $12 and $10 per thousand impressions by position.[^57] Google priced the impression too. The auction does not arrive until AdWords Select in February 2002, which is where the next chapter starts.

Hold the scoreboard at the handover in mind. Overture booked $288.1M in 2001. Google's total revenue that year was $86.4M.[^58] The incumbent in paid search was not Google, and the pure-bid auction was not failing.

The auction era inherits a market of about $7.1B, still 48% priced per impression, with the top ten sellers holding 77% of it.[^59] It also inherits the pools that search will actually take. In 2000, counted on the rule the next chapter itself uses, those were classified at $21.9B and direct response at $72.3B, sitting almost entirely in newspapers, mail and phone books.[^60]

## Legends this chapter does not repeat

Three stories usually attach themselves to this era. The record does not support them.

No one banner came first, and nobody audited the click rate quoted from HotWired's launch.

The newest medium was not the biggest one. Newspapers led until 2000 and direct mail led after. Chapter 3 makes the same correction about radio.

And measurable advertising did not begin with the web. Direct mail and directories had been sold on response for decades, and in 2000 they were worth many times the entire internet ad market.
