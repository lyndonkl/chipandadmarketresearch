# Open problems — three choices the human has to make

Internal working document. Exempt from the readability gate. Scores run anyway and reported at the foot.

Seven design agents answered three open problems left by the P2 design pass. This document sets out each answer, what it buys, and what it costs. It names no winner. The human picks.

Read each problem statement first. Each one is written for somebody who has never seen the project.

---

## Problem 1 — The eleven screens about buying the front door

Google won search advertising twice over. It built a better auction, and it paid other companies to make Google the search box people met first. The research holds eleven screens on that second half. They cover the May 2002 AOL deal. They cover the gap between a dollar Google earned on its own page and a dollar it earned on somebody else's. They cover the Google shares handed to AOL that nobody counts. They cover the 2024 court ruling that the whole business rested on paying to be the default. Nobody has drawn those eleven screens. Four of the five architects gave the whole set a sentence or less, and the set-level review calls it the largest build risk in the project.

Two agents answered. One wants the reader to work a second machine. One wants the reader to read a sealed drawing.

### Option 1A — The Door Bench

**What the reader sees and does.** The reader meets the same bench with a different part opened. One drawing carries two lanes into one auction box. The upper lane holds searches on Google's own page. The lower lane enters through a hinged door and holds searches that start on somebody else's page. Both lanes run through the same rule box. Only the lower lane passes a valve on the way out, and that valve taps most of the money back down and out through the door. Brass dots travel both lanes at the filed 2002 ratio, three on the top for every one on the bottom. Three open cups stand on one baseline: what goes back out the door, what answering the search costs, and what Google keeps. One handle sits under the drawing. The handle is a wheel with six notches, and every notch is a number the record actually contains. The reader drags it from 0 to 91 per cent, watches the brass cup empty, watches the profit needle cross zero into the negative, and watches the door swing open to Google anyway. Eleven stops run in three acts.

**What it teaches.** A dollar arrives and splits. The eye computes that split without being told, because nine countable dots falling down a tap and one carrying on is not a picture of a 91 per cent revenue share — it is the revenue share. Three cups on one baseline turn the answer into a length, and the reader knows the brass cup is nearly empty before reading a numeral. The first stop hands the reader the wheel and asks for 85 per cent, so the reader produces 15 cents before the page shows the filed 21.3. The design draws the famous $100m guarantee as a band from $75m to $150m with no middle mark, because the record's range is wider than the rule allows a point to stand for. A stippled pipe carrying the one economic defence nobody ever measured stays visible in all eleven states, so the reader never sees a complete machine.

**What it costs.** Twelve to fifteen working days. The widest object in the project: 940 by 700 drawing units plus a 320-pixel sticky column. Eleven stops is a long walk, and a reader who leaves at stop three keeps two facts. On a phone the design admits it becomes a demonstration rather than an instrument. The design gives up the century — the reader gets no sense of the $400bn market these numbers sit inside. It also privileges AOL over every other partner, because AOL is the only partner with filed traces.

**Why you would pick it.** The loss-leading trade is a feeling before it is a fact, and the only way a reader feels it is with a hand on the wheel.

**The sharpest argument against it.** The wheel tells the reader that Google set the take rate. The record says the opposite: break B4 states that the rate was an auction outcome, and partners bid it up and back down. The design knows this, names it as its own sharpest cost, and answers with three printed guards — a label, an engraved housing line, and a fixed rival ceiling. Every other page in this project argues that a percept beats a caption. That argument does not stop applying here. The gesture says "you set this" and three captions fight it. The build also spends a second full apparatus, twelve to fifteen days, immediately after the auction bench, which the review already prices at 35 to 45 per cent of one chapter.

### Option 1B — The Door Plate

**What the reader sees and does.** The reader meets one large engineering plate, zoned A to E. Zone A draws the front door and the rent Google paid for it. Zone B shows who could afford that door, with Google's ceiling and the rival's ceiling drawn as unfilled outlines because both figures are teaching inventions. Zone C draws the two pipes behind the door, the same auction running both, and the two rust valves biting them at very different widths. Zone D shows what the partner pipe actually added. Zone E shows the equity leg nobody counts. Along the bottom runs a control strip in exactly the position, size and slot count of the auction bench's control strip — and every slot is empty. A stamped plate sits where each handle should be, reading the value and where it came from. A line beneath reads NOTHING TURNS HERE. Three detail views and three printed tables carry the arithmetic. The whole exhibit moves for under three seconds, once, then stands still forever.

**What it teaches.** The missing handle is the lesson. A reader who has cranked five machines has learned that a knob means somebody chose this number. Putting a knob under $654.7m of filed cost imports that meaning onto a number nobody chose. The empty slot inverts it: Google's engineers chose the auction's rules, and nobody chose these — two firms negotiated them in private, filed them after the fact, and in AOL's case never filed them at all. The design also protects the auction bench. THE BAND is the chapter's peak and its most expensive interaction, and a second operable machine directly behind it competes with that peak for memory. The one comparison that deserves the most accurate encoding gets it: 95.5 cents against 21.3 cents, two lengths from one baseline. Everything the record cannot rank gets a deliberately weaker drawing — a span with no middle, two marks with a labelled gap, a stipple block with a name.

**What it costs.** Eight to nine working days. Roughly three and a half screens of scroll, because a plate must show all eleven scenarios at once where a simulator reuses one frame. About 600 words of on-screen text plus 350 of prose, and the plate punishes skimming. The design forfeits discovery on all eleven scenarios. It loses two genuinely good interactions: dragging the rival's revenue up until the winner flips, and moving the share price to watch the equity leg cost more in exactly the states where Google wins.

**Why you would pick it.** A filed number should never sit under a slider, and the empty slot says that louder than any caption.

**The sharpest argument against it.** The sealed control strip only lands for a reader who learned the auction bench, remembers where its six handles sat, and notices that this strip holds plates instead. Every other reader sees six broken knobs. The design's own kill switch admits this and offers to drop the beat entirely. The plate also hands the reader nothing to do at the exact point in the chapter where the argument needs them awake. It then delivers its case as 600 words of on-screen text. A skimmer will walk straight past that with four numbers.

### The choice

**Does the reader need a hand on this material, or does a filed number lose more by sitting under a handle than the reader gains by moving it?**

## Problem 2 — Seven eras, seven middlemen, seven different bottom numbers

A middleman sits between the advertiser and the place the advertisement appears, and keeps a slice of the money. Every era wrote that slice down as a percentage — but a percentage of what changed seven times. Era 1 counted 15 per cent of everything the advertiser handed over. Era 2 counted 73 per cent of money that had already lost the agency's 15 per cent. Era 5 counted one company's own profit margin. Era 7 counted three studies that disagree and cannot be reconciled. Put seven bars in a row and the reader compares 15 to 85 and concludes the middleman's cut grew six-fold. Nothing in the record says that. What the record says is narrower and better. For a hundred and fifty years somebody outside counted the cut, and counted it against the advertiser's money. After 1993 the sellers counted it against their own books. That is the same break, in the same decade, as the break in the audience count.

Three agents answered. All three refuse the seven-bar chart. They differ on how they stop it.

### Option 2A — The Fitting Bench

**What the reader sees and does.** The reader meets one iron channel labelled EVERY $100 THE ADVERTISER HANDED OVER, drawn the same width in every drawing on the page. Seven ports hang off it, one per era, and the ports come in four physically different fittings, because the seven numbers were measured on four different kinds of money. The reader then picks up a single valve — the one cut for the advertiser's dollar — and tries to bolt it into each era's port. Where the port matches, the valve seats and a cup fills. Where it does not, the valve travels most of the way down and stops dead, six pixels short, and a red clearance dimension snaps in between the stub and the bore: THIS VALVE DOES NOT FIT THIS PIPE. Below the fitting test sits the payoff board: seven gauge glasses on a bench floor that steps down at 1994, the upper floor labelled COUNTED AGAINST THE ADVERTISER'S DOLLAR and the lower floor COUNTED AGAINST THE SELLER'S OWN BOOKS.

**What it teaches.** The reader attempts the forbidden comparison and the machine physically refuses it. A reader who is warned about bottom numbers forgets. A reader who reaches for the wrong pipe and feels the valve stop dead has made the error personally and had it corrected in about a second. The design opens on era 3 on purpose, because era 3 carries two middlemen on two different bases inside one decade, so the reader cannot retreat to "well, times changed" — the lesson lands on measurement rather than on history. The gauge floor then steps down the encoding hierarchy deliberately: two floors, per-gauge zeroes, per-gauge scales, which makes reading inside a gauge cheap and reading across gauges expensive. That is the exact cost structure the evidence supports. The floor step itself carries the 1994 finding and rhymes with the audience-count thread instead of digressing from it.

**What it costs.** About five days, against half a day for seven bars in a row. Two of the three boards need 940 pixels and scroll sideways below that. The reader spends 60 to 75 seconds of real attention before the section pays anything back. The design gives up any single line for "the middleman's cut over 180 years", and some readers will feel withheld from. Its upper floor still invites a comparison of eras 1 to 4, and the design names that as its weakest joint. Era 2's gauge is a stack, the one place the house rule bends. The refusal — the best thing in the design — needs a rocker, which means it is desktop-first.

**Why you would pick it.** The reader learns the rule by breaking it, and nothing else in the project teaches by refusal.

**The sharpest argument against it.** The best moment in this design costs four crank operations on two rockers, and the design concedes that most phone readers will never perform them. Those readers get board one and a table — most of the facts and none of the argument. The fitting metaphor also asks the reader to learn four port shapes before anything pays back, which is a second vocabulary inside a chapter that has already taught one.

### Option 2B — The Base Plate and the Rack

**What the reader sees and does.** The reader meets seven valves at seven fixed positions, one per era, each tapping money down into its own column. At rest the seven columns hang over seven short separate rulers with seven different units printed under them. Nothing shares a scale and nothing lines up. An empty iron shelf labelled RACK sits above. The reader then fits a base plate by pressing one of three stops: each era's own base, the advertiser's dollar, or the middleman's own books. The machine re-plumbs itself in about a second. Eras that can be rewritten onto that base lower their pipes and fill from a shared zero line. Eras that cannot swing up out of the machine, land on the shelf directly above their own column, cap their outlets, and print one plain sentence saying why. The rack counter reads 3 OF 7. The reader presses the other plate and the parts swap. No plate ever holds all seven.

**What it teaches.** Every reader arrives believing the seven percentages are comparable. Pressing "the advertiser's dollar" is the reader stating that belief out loud, and three parts lifting off the machine refutes a belief the reader owns. Two plates then defeat the misreading one plate cannot fix. One plate with three drop-outs teaches that three eras have bad numbers. Two plates where the drop-outs swap teach that the numbers are fine and the questions differ — era 2 refuses the advertiser's dollar and then fits the middleman's books cleanly at 73 cents on audited federal figures. A part that lands on a labelled shelf and prints a reason is an object with a location and a name, where a missing bar is a hole and holes get forgotten.

**What it costs.** Three to five days, and the design flags the swing transform alone as a full day whose first version should be thrown away. One full screen and part of another; it does not compress, because cutting the rack destroys the argument. Two presses minimum to reach the finding, three to reach it properly. The design refuses a time series on purpose, and readers will want one badly. Three real numbers get demoted to the printed table because they sit on neither plate.

**Why you would pick it.** The parts coming off and going back on is the clearest single picture of what "different bottom numbers" means.

**The sharpest argument against it.** Everything rests on the second press. A reader who presses once and scrolls away learns that three eras have bad research — the opposite of the finding. The design's own fallback is to auto-run the second plate after three seconds, which takes back the agency the whole design was built on. The design also draws four bars on one shared scale, which is the exact percept the problem statement calls dangerous, licensed by an act most readers will not perform.

### Option 2C — The Toll Plate

**What the reader sees and does.** The reader meets seven small, separately framed machine drawings, one per era, each one the toll organ lifted off that era's bench. In every drawing a brass bar enters from the left, a valve diverts a rust wedge down into a cup, and the rest leaves by a spout. The bar is always exactly the same length, because the bar is always the whole of that era's own starting amount. The line directly above each drawing names that starting amount in a plain sentence, and that sentence differs on every card. Under each drawing sit three fixed rows: THE CUT, OF WHAT, and WHO SAYS SO. The cards drop one grid square from their neighbours, so no two bars ever sit on the same horizontal line and nothing on the page can be read across. Era 7 takes the whole final row, and its cup is the only one that cannot be filled shut: the top block is stipple with a ragged open edge reading NOBODY COULD SAY WHERE THIS WENT · 15%. The one thing the reader is invited to read down the page is the bottom row of every card — who counted the cut.

**What it teaches.** Small multiples normally work because the panels share a scale. These panels must not, so the design strips the three cues that make small multiples read as one series: the shared baseline goes, the common axis goes, and the shared caption goes. What is left to compare then moves to where comparison is cheap — a fixed column at a fixed height, read top to bottom, which is the most accurate judgment the eye makes and costs almost nothing. Era 7's unclosable cup pops out before any word is decoded, and it is the default state rather than something gated behind a control. The design also hands thread 2's cross-era job to thread 1's ladder, so two threads reach the same 1990s collapse by different evidence.

**What it costs.** Two to three days on top of the shared primitives, four to five without them. About 1,290 pixels of desktop page and about 2,900 on a phone, for a thread that yields no series. Seven title blocks means seven serial reads, and the design admits its five-second read is thin. It gives up the sentence "the middleman's cut rose" or "fell" entirely. No single screenshot carries the argument, and era 7's ragged cup on its own can be misread as exactly the claim the page refuses to make.

**Why you would pick it.** The finding arrives without the reader touching anything, which is the only version most readers will actually get.

**The sharpest argument against it.** The plate never lets the reader test the comparison, so a reader who arrives believing the seven numbers are comparable leaves with that belief intact and seven unconnected facts. The design spends three times the page of a chart to say something smaller. Readers who came to learn how much middlemen take get seven answers and no summary, and some will read that as evasion rather than rigour. The counter glyph also asks the reader to learn a second small ordinal scale in a chapter that already carries one.

### The choice

**Should the page stop the wrong comparison by letting the reader attempt it and fail, by handing back the parts that do not fit, or by making sure nothing ever lines up in the first place?**

A second, smaller question rides on it: does the argument have to survive on a phone, where only the third option fully does?

## Problem 3 — The comparison tool nobody will find

The project's main analytical tool lifts one part out of all seven machines and lines the seven copies up in date order. A reader can compare who counted the audience in 1914 with who counted it in 2021, side by side, in one glance. Today the reader reaches it one way only: by clicking a small grey label that does not look clickable. The page has spent its whole first screen teaching that everything here is a drawing rather than a control. All five architects invented the tool and all five predicted readers would miss it. A reader can finish all seven chapters, understand every machine, and never once see the arc the essay exists to deliver.

Two agents answered. One makes the tool look operable. One removes the need to find it.

### Option 3A — The Pull Ring

**What the reader sees and does.** The design redraws every part as a cartridge seated in the frame, with a visible seam down each side and a small knurled pull ring on its corner — the object anyone who has opened a filing drawer already understands. The rings sit at eight frozen positions, identical in all seven machines. The reader meets them on the first screen. At the end of chapter 1 the prose plants a question the reader now wants answered. The meter's ring then tugs itself ten pixels and springs back, once. The first pull answers the essay's thesis in one glance: seven counters in date order, each hanging at its distance from the seller, deep for eighty-two years and flush against the seller at the end. From then on the drawer never shuts all the way. A 26-pixel lip stays at the bottom of every screen, naming the last part pulled and carrying eight small squares — one per part, filled as the reader opens them.

**What it teaches.** A handle is understood from the world; an underline is understood from the web. This page has spent its first screen teaching the opposite of web convention, so importing button styling would either be ignored or would wreck the register. A small knurled circle on a page of straight iron segments is the only shape of its kind, which means the eye finds it in parallel rather than by hunting. The tug then demonstrates rather than instructs, and it borrows the crank's exact 40-millisecond pause, so the reader files the pull as the same machine answering the same hand. The first payoff does the real teaching: a reader whose own first action delivers the essay's whole argument forms an intention to pull again. The lip afterwards converts remembering into recognising, and eight squares with three filled leaves a standing itch to fill the rest.

**What it costs.** Twenty-six pixels of every screen, forever, after the first pull. Roughly 24 by 18 pixels of ring inside every machine drawing, eight times, in the drawing's busiest register. Four to six seconds of instruction, landing in chapter 1, where the direction already spends about ninety seconds teaching the bench. About a day for the rings and their states; the lip touches every page's bottom padding, and the teaching sequence is a four-state machine with four ways to be wrong and needs its own test page. The design adds a second verb, PULL, beside CRANK, and the direction's cleanest claim was that it has one verb. The lip is also chrome, on a page whose aesthetic argument is that it carries none.

**Why you would pick it.** The tool becomes visible, permanently labelled, and one click away, without the page having to spend half the screen on it.

**The sharpest argument against it.** A visible handle is not a used handle. Every reader who does not pull sees the same seven chapters they see today, plus eight rings of extra ink on every drawing and 26 pixels of chrome forever. The design also concedes that everything rests on the first meter strip being the best eight seconds in chapter 1. A reader who pulls once and finds the payoff thin will never pull again. No amount of signposting recovers that reader.

### Option 3B — The Shelf

**What the reader sees and does.** Every workbench has a lower shelf. This one holds seven slots, one per era, left to right, drawn as part of the bench and always on screen. Each slot holds a small drawing of whichever part the reader is currently reading about. The reader scrolls from the paragraph about who counted to the paragraph about how the price was set, and all seven slots restock at once — seven meters become seven pricing rules, in the same seven positions. The reader clicks nothing. Then, once per chapter, at the beat where the argument turns, the prose says "put that on the shelf beside the machines before it". The shelf rises. The machine slides up to a thin stub, the seven slots grow into full cards with captions, and the reader reads 180 years of that one part in one screen. The part that rises is always the meter, because the essay's spine is the counting discipline. Seven chapters, seven raises, one more slot filled each time.

**What it teaches.** Comparison across seven items is simultaneity or it is nothing, because visual working memory holds about four things. Today the seven eras sit far apart down the page, so comparing them is a memory task performed across scroll events. Putting all seven in one field turns remembering into seeing. Discovery filters on curiosity rather than on need, and the reader who most needs the comparison is the least likely to go looking for it. A static strip would go invisible by chapter 2, but this strip restocks as the reader's own scrolling brings each part into view, so every change re-enters attention and inherits it rather than competing for it. Seven blocks hanging below one shared line, then rising to it and staying, lands before a single word is read. Seven raises of the same rack is spaced repetition of one structure, and the structure is the thesis.

**What it costs.** Four hundred and eighty pixels of every viewport, permanently, on all seven era chapters — 53 per cent of a 900-pixel screen, leaving about thirteen lines of prose. The design cannot be dismissed, because a dismissible shelf recreates the problem it fixes. It displaces about 104 pixels of machine height (the bench must be redrawn smaller, not scaled), about three lines of prose per scroll step, the end-of-chapter summary board, and the 56-cell reference grid's job. It needs 56 hand-authored part drawings, one per era per part, roughly 28 hours of drawing, plus three days of state machine and axis work. Six to seven working days. The meter is privileged in all seven chapters; the other seven parts are only ever seen small. Four of the eight parts get no shared scale at all, because they change in kind rather than in amount. On a phone the shelf collapses to a 34-pixel position rule.

**Why you would pick it.** The comparison arrives whether or not the reader ever asks for it, which is the only way the incurious reader ever sees the arc.

**The sharpest argument against it.** The shelf takes half the screen on every era chapter and hands that space to one part out of eight. A reader who came for prose loses three lines per scroll step for seven chapters and cannot turn it off. The stacked axes are a real confusion risk — the machine's horizontal axis means money flowing, the shelf's means time passing, and the design names this as the thing most likely to break. On a phone the whole apparatus becomes a 34-pixel position rule, so the phone reader gets the comparison as a destination seven times and never as an ambient fact. That is the same failure this brief exists to fix, moved to a different device.

### The choice

**Should the page make the comparison tool findable, or make it unavoidable?**

The cost sits on the other side of that question: 26 pixels forever and a second verb, or 480 pixels forever and 56 hand-drawn parts.

## What all of them agree on

Seven agents worked separately on three unrelated problems. They agree on more than they disagree on, and the agreements are worth reading as settled ground.

**All seven keep the Bench.** Not one proposes a new visual language, a new palette, or a new metaphor. Every design uses iron line work on bone paper, the same five colour meanings (brass is money, rust is the take, cyan is the count, iron is mechanism, stipple is documented absence), and the same four type roles. Every one reuses the four named motion curves by name and by value.

**All seven draw missing data as a named object.** Not one leaves white space where a number should be. A stipple block with a printed reason appears in every single proposal, and three of them argue independently that a blank reads as zero while stipple reads as unmeasured.

**All seven obey the span-only rule without special pleading.** Any claim whose range is wider than 60 per cent of its middle value gets drawn as a span with no centre mark. Four designs independently flagged the same claims: the pre-1890s agent commission, the era 3 station-rep cut, the DoubleClick margin, and the $100m AOL guarantee. The most-quoted number in the whole distribution story turns out to be undrawable as a point, and two designs treat that refusal as their best teaching moment rather than as a limitation.

**All seven refuse a free slider on a number nobody could set.** Every control in every proposal is a rocker with named stops, a stepper, or a wheel with notches taken from the record. Two designs go further and stamp the reader's own settings as not filed.

**All seven print every value that would otherwise be a tooltip.** Not one design relies on hover, and every one drops hover entirely on a phone. Every proposal also ships a real printed table beside or below the drawing, so a reader who operates nothing still leaves with the whole record.

**All seven build a genuine alternative for readers with motion turned off.** Not one merely disables the animation. Each proposal names what the motion carried and states what static mark carries it instead — frozen particles with direction arrows, a printed aperture value, two static bars with a caliper between them, a printed year under each cell.

**All seven agree that a caption cannot beat a picture.** All three problem-2 designs reject the direction's own seven-bar chart with its per-bar labels, and all three reject it on the direction's own logic: the eye builds the comparison before it reads the warning underneath. The same argument drives the pull ring (a styled label loses to a drawn handle) and the shelf (a sentence inviting exploration loses to a shelf already holding the parts).

**All seven name their own worst risk in plain words.** Every proposal states what it gives up, and three carry an explicit kill switch or a named fallback if reader testing goes against them. None of them oversells.

**On problem 2, all three agree what the finding actually is.** The finding is not the size of the cut. The finding is who did the counting, and that the counting changed hands in the mid-1990s — the same break, in the same decade, as the break in the audience count. All three designs put that sentence at the bottom of the exhibit and treat the numbers above it as evidence for it.

**Every proposal costs more than the obvious answer, and every proposal says so.** The cheapest of the seven runs two to three days. The dearest runs twelve to fifteen. Nobody proposed the cheap version of anything, and nobody hid the bill.

## The three questions, together

1. **Problem 1.** Does the reader need a hand on the distribution engine, or does a filed number lose more by sitting under a handle than the reader gains by moving it?
2. **Problem 2.** Should the page stop the wrong comparison by letting the reader attempt it and fail, by handing back the parts that do not fit, or by making sure nothing ever lines up?
3. **Problem 3.** Should the page make the comparison tool findable, or make it unavoidable?
